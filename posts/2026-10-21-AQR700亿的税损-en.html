AQR's $70B Tax Strategy: Schwab Raised the Minimum from $1M to $10M

2026-10-21 · Ken Zhang / Gravitas Recruitment

Start with a contrast. AQR's tax-aware long-short strategy ran about $3 billion in 2023. It now runs roughly $70 billion — 23x in three years. Over the same stretch, Charles Schwab — which custodies much of it — raised the minimum on some new accounts from $1 million to $10 million.

One side is a quant giant compounding 23x. The other side is the distribution channel applying the brakes.

The money is flowing somewhere that has very little to do with factor research.

What the strategy actually does

Tax-aware long-short is not a stock-picking strategy. It is tax engineering. Both legs get levered — a common structure is 140/40, with 140% long and 40% short, roughly unchanged net exposure, but both books keep realizing losses that can be harvested against gains.

One example from AQR's own materials: $100 million in its most aggressive Flex strategy can generate more than $580 million of tax deductible losses over ten years, while the portfolio itself still rises. A separate product, Delphi Plus, is built specifically to generate a steady loss stream that offsets ordinary income.

Brent Sullivan of Tax Alpha Insider estimates that firms including AQR and Quantinno ran a little over $1 billion in these strategies five years ago. Today the category is close to $190 billion. In Bloomberg's Big Take of August 3, AQR's own slice went from about $3 billion in 2023 to roughly $70 billion, while the firm's total hedge fund assets crossed $140 billion at the end of Q1 2026.

Why the brakes now

Because the leverage and the operational risk accumulated on the custodian's side of the table.

Schwab's Q3 report is blunt about it: $188 billion in margin loan balances, nearly double year on year, of which around $61 billion relates to these tax-aware long-short strategies. CEO Rick Wurster described adoption growth this year as "unusually fast."

Fidelity moved first — in February it stopped opening accounts for new clients. Money went to Schwab. Schwab then set its own limits: minimums on some new accounts from $1 million to $10 million, portfolio margin accounts closed to new clients and new money, and a 30% cap on how much of an advisor's assets can sit in these products. Both firms are tightening while money keeps arriving.

US margin debt reached $1.45 trillion in August on FINRA's measure, close to the $1.5 trillion record set earlier in the summer. Regulators have noticed — a US Treasury official, speaking in New York in July, said some products designed to compress tax liability may have crossed a line they should not cross. No rule has been issued.

What this has to do with quant careers

The connection is in the job structure, not in the strategy itself.

The substance of AQR's business here is high-frequency daily rebalancing, securities borrowing, a large short book, and tax filings derived from a lot of complexity. Behind a single account sit thousands of positions and hundreds of pages of tax documents. That workload concentrates demand in two places: engineers doing execution and portfolio optimisation, and back- and middle-office people doing tax, compliance and operations.

That map looks different from the one in China. Operations roles at domestic private funds centre on NAV calculation, unit registration and regulatory reporting. In the US version of this product, tax engineering has become a core link in the research chain — it even shapes the strategy structure in reverse.

AQR itself treats this as a systematic problem. Its 2026 series "Misconceptions about Tax-Aware Long-Short" responds to challenges such as how the approach compares with direct indexing. The central argument: the right frame is a trade-off between pre-tax alpha and after-tax outcomes, not maximising realised losses.

My read is that the moat in this business is not in the factors. It is in the channel and the balance sheet. Anyone can build the strategy. Being able to get the broker's financing and the borrow, and not blowing up when everyone unwinds at once, is the actual barrier. That is also why the one applying the brakes is Schwab, not AQR.

(A note for candidates: this business has no equivalent soil in mainland China — the tax code differs, the securities lending market differs. But if your direction is cross-border product operations, clearing, or prime brokerage coverage, this is exactly where these firms have been expanding for three years.)

One thing worth watching

AQR's 23x did not come from stronger excess returns. It came from finding a reason clients have to buy — tax. That is rare in this industry: scale usually follows performance. This time it followed the tax code.

The open question is whether the strategy is as safe at $70 billion as it was at $3 billion. Short books getting more concentrated, unwinds getting more synchronised — those are the things custodians are being asked about on quarterly earnings calls.
