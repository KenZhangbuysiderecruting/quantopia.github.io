Poesis, Ten People: The Fund Where the AI Places the Orders

2026-11-15 · Ken Zhang / Gravitas Recruitment

Ten people. Eighty to a hundred stocks. The AI decides what to buy and when to sell. The humans do two things: set the risk parameters, and click approve.

Bloomberg reported on 10 November that Alex Popa and Charles Elkan have founded Poesis in Menlo Park, California. Popa spent more than fifteen years at Capital Group, where he managed its US growth fund; Elkan previously ran machine learning at Goldman Sachs. The first strategy, AI Clarity, has run the founders' own money since August and is due to open to outside investors in early 2027.

First, separate productivity from authority

For two years, when quant firms talked about AI they meant productivity: large models to read the literature, generate factor candidates, help write code, speed up backtests. The human's position did not change. Humans decided what to do; models helped do it faster.

Poesis describes something else. AI agents use quantitative models to research, identify opportunities and decide trades; humans set risk parameters and approve. The operative word is approve, not decide.

One detail shows how far that goes: when the agents detect a potential downturn, the system shorts equity indices to hedge the exposure. That action is written into the strategy logic, not chosen by a person in the moment.

So this is not "AI helps the researcher research". It is "the researcher has been removed from the trading decision chain".

A three-year holding period is the counterintuitive part

Eighty to a hundred stocks, held for three years. That structure drives turnover to a minimum — a different species from the daily and weekly index-enhanced products common in China.

It looks more like disciplined active management, with the stock picker swapped from a portfolio manager to a model. Which explains why the founders are an active PM and a machine learning head rather than two high-frequency traders.

The implication for compute is equally counterintuitive. A three-year horizon means the model does not need microseconds; it needs label quality, clean fundamental and supply-chain data, and an evaluation framework that keeps the model from being dragged off course by noise over three years. That technical path diverges from the mid- and high-frequency route — it is expensive in data, not in hardware.

Seventy basis points, more honest than any pitch

In October, AI Clarity fell 0.3%. The S&P 500 fell about 1%. It outperformed by 0.7 points.

Those 0.7 points did not come from earning more. They came from losing less, because the system hedged when it detected downside risk. For a product that has only run founders' money so far, that number is more honest than any backtest: the logic did not break in a live market, and its return currently comes mostly from risk control rather than stock selection.

The stated goal is to beat the broader market while limiting major drawdowns. The order matters — limit the drawdown first, beat the market second.

Ten people against several thousand, used carefully

Bloomberg notes the AI-led operating model could let the firm run billions with fewer than 100 employees, well below the staffing of platform multistrategy firms such as Citadel and Millennium.

The comparison holds, but only so far. A large share of headcount at platform firms sits in compliance, operations, middle and back office, plus the research teams of dozens of independent pods. What those firms sell is multi-strategy, multi-pod diversification — not the same product as a single-strategy AI fund.

The fair comparison is operating cost per unit of assets. Ten people running several billion produces a startling assets-per-head figure; it also means that when any link fails, there is no second person behind it. That is an efficiency structure and a fragility structure at once.

What is left of the research job: three things

If the decision moves to the model, what remains for researchers? From this structure, three things.

One, frame the problem. Which variables enter the model, how labels are defined, what horizon is being predicted. These choices decide what the model can and cannot see.

Two, accept the output. Whether the resulting book makes sense on sector spread, concentration, turnover and correlation to macro variables. That requires a human with more common sense than the model.

Three, handle what the model cannot explain. When it is heavy in a stock a person finds alarming, someone has to judge whether the model sees a signal others missed or the data is broken.

None of the three generates alpha. All three can destroy it. In China these duties are rarely separate roles; they sit inside "quantitative researcher" as the senior half of the job. When a firm genuinely hands over the decision, they become separate seats.

One thing Bloomberg does not say: whether the "short indices on detected downside" rule was written by a human or learned by the model. That determines whether "autonomous" is the right word. If a human wrote it, the system is really human-authored risk control plus model stock selection — considerably more conservative than the pitch. If the model learned it, its behaviour in a market state it has never seen is unpredictable.

There is no answer yet. It is the first question worth asking when the fund opens in early 2027.

How many people on your team do model acceptance full time — not writing factors, not running backtests, just judging whether the book the model hands you makes sense? The count is the datapoint.
