AI Didn't Eliminate Quant Researchers — It Just Devalued "Can Code"

2026-08-23 · Ken Zhang / Gravitas Recruitment

For the past three years, the bear case on quant researchers has never let up. AI can write code, read filings and research reports, and run backtests on its own — plenty of people have been waiting for the role to disappear.

So what happened? Research teams at Citadel, Two Sigma, D.E. Shaw and Jane Street have grown, not shrunk. This year, Citadel Securities hired more than 60 people across six Asian markets in one go, with nearly half landing in Hong Kong, and said explicitly it was "focusing on researchers, engineers and traders." That data came from Bloomberg on May 21 — a primary source.

On one side, AI keeps getting more capable; on the other, hiring keeps getting more aggressive. What is the disconnect? It is in who exactly is being hired.

Hiring Is Still Expanding, but the Bar Has Moved

Citadel Securities' head of Asia-Pacific, Vikesh Kotecha, put it in diplomatic terms; translated plainly: in the Hong Kong talent pool, they are not looking for people who can write scripts — they want researchers and engineers who can carry the market-making business.

The more interesting line is another one. According to media reports, Citadel Securities' chief human resources officer, Alexander DiLeonardo, made a point this year: because large models have lowered the bar on everyday coding, the firm now "hires managers from day one." The weight of purely technical programming tests is coming down, replaced by "meta-skills" — the ability to spot problems, business judgment, cross-functional collaboration, and the knack for orchestrating people and AI into a single system.

(That "hiring managers from day one" line is a media relay — I could not find the Citadel original, so treat it as a signal, not gospel.)

Coding Is Depreciating; Problem-Framing Is Appreciating

Put plainly, the change boils down to one sentence: execution has been commoditised; judgment has not.

AI can reproduce a hundred factors, but which factor will decay in the A-share market, why it decays, and what to replace it with — that is still a human job. Models can generate backtest reports, but whether a backtest is overfitted, whether it can go live, and whether to cut it when it is losing — someone still has to make the call.

Which also explains why top firms are deploying AI platforms while still adding researchers: AI makes each individual researcher more productive, but "forming a hypothesis, designing an experiment, judging a result" — that is something AI cannot replace.

The Resume Changes I See as a Headhunter

On the recruiting side, I see this directly. For the same quant researcher role, three years ago we screened resumes by language and framework first; this year we look at two things first: whether the person has real projects where they used AI tools to do the work, and how much of their output is their own judgment versus shuttle-service from a model.

Writing "proficient in AI-assisted research" on a resume has gone from a plus to a prerequisite. Conversely, the thing that commands a price now is being able to explain: "what I had AI run, where I did not trust it, and how I changed it in the end."

This has little to do with AI per se and everything to do with an old rule: the cheaper the tools, the more expensive the judgment.

Two Straight Words for Those Still Grinding

If you are preparing to enter this industry, stop grinding leetcode-style drills. Probability problems and C++ syntax — AI drills those better than you.

Put your energy into two things. One: learn to give AI the right instructions and to spot in a glance where it is hallucinating — that is worth more than hand-writing a factor. Two: build one or two complete projects of the "I define the problem, AI executes, I verify" kind, so you can walk through your judgment process in interviews.

People who can code are a dime a dozen. People who can define what should be computed have always been scarce.
