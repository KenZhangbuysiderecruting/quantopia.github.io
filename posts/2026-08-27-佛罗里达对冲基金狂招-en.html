Florida Hedge Funds Are Binge-Hiring Fresh PhDs: The Quant Talent Pipeline Is Changing

2026-08-27 · Ken Zhang / Gravitas Recruitment

Quant hedge funds poaching talent is hardly news, but the direction of the poaching is shifting. In early August, US talent analytics firm The Kronor Group published a report: Florida's quant hedge funds are pulling freshly minted PhDs straight into the front office — from theoretical physics to statistics, from MIT to UC Berkeley.

This is not a one-off at a single firm; it is a talent pipeline taking shape.

Four New Faces, Four New Signals

In July, WorldQuant hired Jacky Yip, a PhD in theoretical physics from the University of Wisconsin–Madison, as an AI scientist. The same firm also moved Janka Möller-Wilhelmy from its Swiss office to Florida with a promotion to senior AI scientist. She had only completed her mathematics PhD at the University of Vienna in 2024, specialising in machine learning within mathematical finance.

In May, Schonfeld hired Willie McClinton, a PhD in electrical engineering and computer science from MIT, as a quantitative researcher. He had already interned at Schonfeld before receiving his full-time offer.

In January, Florida-based Voloridge hired Georgios Fanaras, a PhD in theoretical physics and quantum cosmology from Tufts University, who had interned at Voloridge in summer 2025.

In February, Cubist, under Point72, hired Mriganka Basu Roy Chowdhury, a statistics PhD from UC Berkeley. His 2025 internship was at Citadel Securities.

Five people, five schools, five specialisations. The common thread: all went straight into front-office research roles around graduation.

Intern-to-Full-Time Conversion Is Becoming the Main Channel

The most notable thing in this report is not the degrees — it is the path. All three new hires at Schonfeld, Voloridge and Cubist have internship experience "at the same firm or a peer." McClinton interned at Schonfeld, Fanaras at Voloridge, and Chowdhury at Citadel Securities.

What does this mean? Quant hedge funds are recruiting more and more like tech companies' campus hiring: intern first, then a return offer, then straight into the job after graduation. For employers, this is lower-risk than poaching a seasoned researcher from Wall Street — you have already seen how they code, how they do research and how they fit into a team. For students, it is a more certain career path.

According to The Kronor Group's report, early-career talent (typically within three years of graduation) has risen from about 20% of Florida hedge fund hires in 2023 to about 30% in 2025. It does not sound like much, but a 10-percentage-point shift in two years is a fast migration within a talent structure.

(Note: the share figures come from The Kronor Group / AMObserver coverage — a media relay, with no primary source disclosed.)

Why Florida?

Look at the map. WorldQuant is headquartered in Connecticut and Schonfeld in New York, but their Florida offices are expanding. Citadel Securities is based in Miami, and Voloridge sits in Jupiter. Florida has no state income tax and a lower cost of living, which appeals to young researchers; for funds, operating costs are more controllable too.

More importantly, the physical location of quant research no longer matters much. With data, compute and risk infrastructure in place, a PhD researcher can run global strategies from Palm Beach. Florida is exploiting that window, turning itself from a retirement paradise into a new hub for quant talent.

Implications for China and Asia

This trend carries two signals for Asia's quant industry.

First, the talent race has moved earlier. Chinese quant funds have spent the past two or three years fighting over seasoned PMs and senior researchers, but overseas platforms are already planting internship relationships as early as a PhD student's first or second year. By graduation, employers are already queued up.

Second, disciplinary boundaries are blurring. Yip is theoretical physics, Fanaras is quantum cosmology, Möller-Wilhelmy is machine learning in mathematical finance — none of these are traditional finance backgrounds. Quant's definition of a "relevant major" keeps widening; what is genuinely scarce is the ability to turn abstract math and physics problems into tradable signals.

For a headhunter, this means the "candidate profile" clients ask for is changing: no longer just "do they have hedge fund experience?" but "do they have top-tier academic training, an intern-to-conversion trajectory, and the ability to turn a thesis into code?"
