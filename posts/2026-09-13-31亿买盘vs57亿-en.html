$310M of Passive Inflows vs $569M of Supply: India's First MSCI Rebalance Under the New Closing Auction

2026-09-13 · Ken Zhang / Gravitas Recruitment

Mumbai's close on August 31 saw Adani Energy Solutions fall about 10% — its worst single day in roughly seven months. There was no earnings release and no rating downgrade. It was the first major MSCI rebalance since India introduced its new closing-auction mechanism.

A seller nobody named

Turnover in the stock that day was about $569 million. Bloomberg, citing trading data, reported that a single entity sold roughly 21.92 million shares in the closing auction at ₹1,417. The report did not name the seller.

Exchange disclosures offer a clue. GQG Partners' stake in Adani Energy Solutions fell from 5.09% to 3.46%. Between June 30 and August 31, GQG and its concert parties cut about 19.97 million shares, from 62.24 million to 42.30 million. GQG declined to comment; the Adani group did not respond.

The timing overlaps, but the report never confirmed that the block belonged to GQG. That distinction is the one thing worth holding on to when reading this story.

What the arbitrage was betting on

Index-rebalance arbitrage is an old trade. When a stock joins an index, passive money tracking that index must buy on the effective date, and the size of that buying can be estimated in advance. Get in early, sell into the passive flow when it arrives, and the spread is the profit.

Nuvama Alternative & Quantitative Research estimates Adani Energy Solutions would see about $310 million of passive inflows from its inclusion in the MSCI Standard Index, and Adani Enterprises about $202 million from a weight increase.

The model said $310 million of buying. Actual turnover was $569 million. The difference is supply nobody had modelled.

The mechanism changed one variable

The key is that India changed the plumbing. The new closing auction concentrates orders from the end of the session into a single matching window. For passive funds that makes the close more representative of true supply and demand. For arbitrageurs it means every order lands in the same slot.

Liquidity once spread across the final hour is now compressed into minutes. Execution is more efficient — and price elasticity is far larger than the models assume if a large seller picks the same window. That is exactly what happened.

Index arbitrage has long been sold as a low-risk mechanical trade. Only half of it is mechanical: the passive flow. The other half is who else is selling at the same moment.

This is not the A-share playbook

Similar passive-flow arbitrage appeared when A-shares were added to MSCI, but the mechanics differ sharply. India ran a closing auction, and the seller's move only became visible in post-close disclosure — by which point the price had already moved. A-shares use continuous trading into the close, and northbound flows are published daily, so arbitrageurs can adjust intraday.

There is another layer: India discloses large reductions after the close, while A-share major shareholders must pre-announce. A few hours of difference in when information arrives is the difference between two entirely different businesses.

Teams running index events that port a "weight change × passive flow" model into an emerging market usually miss the trading mechanism itself, not a parameter.

The talent profile is shifting too

Index arbitrage and event-driven seats used to be hired on familiarity with index rules and flow models. Now there is a second requirement: understanding local market microstructure — how the auction matches, when disclosure lands, which channel block trades use.

That cannot come from a textbook; it is ground out in a specific market. So when India, Japan or Brazil changes its mechanics, the first people bid for are not the best modellers but the ones who have watched the tape locally.

A question worth asking: does your team model "potential supply in the same window" separately, or only the passive flow?

One line to close

$310 million of certain buying met $569 million of actual turnover. Inside the gap sits the other half of the market — real, but absent from the model.

Index arbitrage was never "certain money." Only the certain half was modelled well enough to make people forget the other half exists.
