AIMA's 20-Point Tax-Gap Letter: Singapore Strikes Back

2026-10-08 · Ken Zhang / Gravitas Recruitment

On 19 August, the Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS) announced three measures, the heaviest of which: profit-related returns that fund managers receive from qualifying funds will be tax-exempt going forward, expected to take effect from the 2027 year of assessment, with details due in the 2027 Budget.

This was not Singapore adding drama on its own initiative. Only ten weeks had passed since Hong Kong gazetted its tax amendment bill on 12 June — the one that takes the effective tax rate on carried interest and performance fees to 0%, and removes the hurdle-rate requirement.

The messenger between the two cities is the Alternative Investment Management Association (AIMA). Its 21 July letter to MAS laid the size of the tax gap and the speed of talent movement on the table.

Put the two hands of cards side by side first.

Performance-split tax rate: Hong Kong 0% (bill through committee, retrospective to YA 2025/26 proposed) | Singapore 24% (exemption announced 19 Aug, effective 2027)

Hurdle rate: Hong Kong removed | Singapore pending the 2027 Budget

Coverage: Hong Kong hedge funds, private credit, digital-asset funds | Singapore qualifying funds 13D / 13O / 13OA / 13U / 13V

Non-tax chips: Hong Kong IPO fundraising USD 45.8bn, AUM HKD 42.2tn | Singapore MAS investing directly in hedge funds, ONE Pass recognising performance income

Hong Kong's Legislative Council returns for a second reading in the second half of the year; Singapore's implementation details are locked in the February 2027 Budget. The time gap is the talent gap.

A few details in AIMA's letter deserve enlarging.

First, an unnamed global head manager disclosed that its headcount in Singapore has been falling in recent years while Hong Kong has grown several-fold — and only a few years ago, Singapore was still its regional centre of gravity. Second, the letter's own words: "personal income tax has become the decisive lever for the most mobile people." Third, roles that do not strictly need to sit in Singapore are now defaulted elsewhere; several global managers have removed Singapore from the candidate list for new regional headquarters. Fourth, the pressure is not only Asian — India's GIFT City is targeting India-linked strategies, and the UAE has assembled a credible offer out of two financial centres.

The association has 2,100 institutional members managing more than USD 2.5 trillion. The list it has opened to MAS on members' behalf is blunt: tax cuts, headcount-linked relief, and clearer channels for bringing in talent. Market observers have done the arithmetic on the two cities' tax gap: if Hong Kong lands its performance-fee exemption, the difference could exceed 20 percentage points.

Pull the camera back, and this tax race is not an Asian specialty at all.

Greece launched a tax programme this summer aimed specifically at hedge fund and private-equity executives. In early September, Chris Rokos, founder of Rokos Capital, moved his tax residence from the UK to Greece — he has long been one of Britain's largest individual taxpayers — and, according to the Financial Times, Millennium is also following the Greek option. In the same week, Millennium was reported to be negotiating a tax arrangement in Geneva, with several Swiss cantons competing for it. Britain's removal of the "non-dom" regime pushes people out; Greece, Switzerland and Dubai reach out to catch them. In the US, an appellate court ruling in early October threatens a self-employment tax structure the hedge fund industry has relied on for years.

The tax residency of high-net-worth individuals has never been so courted.

Hong Kong's confidence is not only about tax. Data published by the SFC in July: Hong Kong's asset and wealth management business reached HKD 42.2 trillion in 2025, up 20% year on year, a record. An early-October report from Touzi supplied the other side: by mid-September, IPO and secondary-listing fundraising in Hong Kong had reached about USD 45.8 billion, nearly double the same period last year; China ETFs exceed RMB 6 trillion. Citi has also run an estimate: Singapore holds about 5,000 fund managers and the mainland about 45,000 — even if only 3% were pulled over by the tax gap, that is 1,500 professionals.

For quant firms, the real hook is the line between the lines: where the liquidity of Chinese assets sits, that is where the trading has to be.

One dark line is still unfinished. Last year Jane Street took six floors of a new Central waterfront landmark at a reported rent of about USD 4 million a month, and Citadel Securities is also expanding its Hong Kong team. In August the FT reported that the government was studying bringing prop-traders' performance pay into the relief; the Financial Services and the Treasury Bureau clarified the same day that proprietary trading does not fit the "fund" definition. But in early October Hong Kong media were still following the thread, and the bureau's latest line is that the relief "is not limited to specific types of companies", with administrative guidance to be issued in due course. Whether that door opens decides whether Hong Kong is recruiting fund managers — or traders.

Tax is a slow variable, but once it starts moving, talent flows like a pendulum. Last round Singapore won on the family-office tax regime and regulatory certainty; this round Hong Kong moved first with 0%, and Singapore answered within ten weeks. The real clash comes in February 2027 — when Singapore's Budget details land, and when Hong Kong's bill returns for its second-reading vote.
