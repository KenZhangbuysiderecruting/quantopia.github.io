20 to 18, 60% to New Managers: How Allocator Money Is Re-Voting

2026-09-17 · Ken Zhang / Gravitas Recruitment

An internal Bank of America report circulated across the industry this week. 321 allocators were surveyed, and several numbers deserve to be pulled out on their own.

First: hedge funds returned 5.5% through July this year, the best first-half performance since 2010. And three years of net outflows reversed — managers raised more capital than expected at the start of the year.

The second set is more counterintuitive: 60% of allocators said they prefer to direct incremental capital to new managers rather than more experienced teams. Vanessa Bogaardt, BofA's head of global capital strategy, put it plainly: "This is not something we've typically seen."

A third cut of the same data: the average number of hedge fund managers an allocator holds fell from 20 a year ago to 18, while the average allocation per manager rose from $42m to $50m. Fewer managers, larger tickets.

Fourth: 62% of investors negotiated capacity rights in 2025, against 17% the year before.

Read those four together and the picture sharpens. Money is concentrating, and at the same time more willing to try unfamiliar names. Capacity — the right to get into a sought-after fund — is becoming the scarce resource allocators compete over.

Why?

Performance is one direct reason. The AI boom lifted equity strategy returns in the first half; long/short equity and multi-manager platforms saw the clearest inflows. Pensions, funds of funds and private banks are all planning to increase hedge fund allocations.

But performance alone does not explain the shift. A 5.5% industry average is a good number, yet convincing allocators to lean less on familiar names and hand money to managers they have never worked with needs a stronger reason than that.

My read: the rise of multi-manager platforms changed what allocators mean by "experience." On a platform built from dozens of independent pods, platform experience and single-PM experience are different things. Allocators increasingly weigh a platform's risk control and capital allocation efficiency over the length of one manager's résumé. Once the platform itself is the credit backing, a new PM on a new platform can price at or above a veteran single-strategy manager.

That also explains why capacity matters so much. Per industry platform AInvest, working from the BofA data, the average count of managers held is falling while per-manager tickets rise. Money concentrates into larger platforms, and those platforms can only absorb so much new capital. The 62% negotiating capacity rights are effectively locking in tickets to good platforms in advance.

Asia-Pacific is becoming a key variable in this reshuffle.

On August 19, the Monetary Authority of Singapore announced a package of measures: a proposed income tax exemption for performance-linked gains from fund management services; a new Hedge Fund Investment Programme (HFIP) with MAS capital committed alongside managers that set up in Singapore; and a new investment management track under the Overseas Networks & Expertise Pass (ONE Pass) that lets performance-linked income count toward pass eligibility.

Three measures, three levers: tax, capital, talent. Singapore's asset management industry reached S$6.7trn (roughly US$5trn) in 2025, growing 7.5% annually over five years. In a parliamentary reply on September 8, MAS said HFIP aims to "anchor global and regional hedge fund managers" and will assess managers' plans to build investment capability in Singapore, including talent development.

Hong Kong is pushing too. The Inland Revenue (Amendment) Bill tabled in June extends carried interest tax concessions to hedge funds, credit funds, real estate funds and digital asset funds, backdated to April 1, 2025. Hong Kong is already one of Asia's largest hedge fund domiciles, with 1,729 limited partnership funds registered as of June 2026.

Singapore and Hong Kong are competing for the role of Asia-Pacific's core hedge fund hub. For allocators, that means more funds to choose from, friendlier tax structures and more flexible talent mobility. For practitioners, Singapore's ONE Pass investment management track is the one to watch — it counts performance-linked income toward pass eligibility, fixing a structural mismatch where traditional work visas only look at fixed salary.

After years in quant headhunting, I have watched a few of these cycles. After 2008 the mood was "safety," money went to established funds with decade-plus track records. After 2020 it was "yield," equity strategies and multi-strategy platforms rose. This one I would call "capacity" — allocators are no longer just chasing the highest return, they are chasing platforms that can absorb large capital steadily while keeping strategic flexibility.

One question worth pressing: when 60% of allocators say they will give a new manager a shot, what counts as new? A two-year-old startup fund, or an independent pod that just received its capital allocation inside a large platform? From what I see, the latter is the overwhelming majority. Genuinely from-scratch funds still struggle to get institutional allocator money.

But that is already enough to change the industry's structure. Platforms are replacing individuals as the first filter allocators apply.

One last question: if you were an allocator holding a $50m hedge fund ticket, would you give it to a single-strategy fund with a 20-year history, or to a five-year-old multi-manager platform with a mature risk framework? There is no right answer, but it is the choice the industry now makes every day.
