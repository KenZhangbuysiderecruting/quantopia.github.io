After 12 Years, Pythagoras Founder Steps Aside: A Harvard Energy Veteran Builds "Electricity Perps" for AI Data Centers

2026-09-23 · Ken Zhang / Gravitas Recruitment

A man who spent twelve years running a crypto hedge fund now wants to take the invention of the perpetual futures contract and apply it to trading electricity.

The name Mitchell Dong barely registers in the Chinese quant world. But his career sits precisely on the seam lines of three eras: first energy, then crypto, now AI. And he has just done something rare in this industry — handed over the crypto hedge fund he founded, Pythagoras Investments, completely.

This was no abrupt changing of the guard. Opalesque obtained two letters he wrote to investors — one from Dong himself, one from the two succeeding partners. Together, the letters pre-answer nearly every question a due-diligence team would ask when a hedge fund changes hands.

Start with the man.

Dong studied economics at Harvard College and went through Harvard Business School's OPM program. He entered the energy industry in 1973 and has spent over half a century in and around it. Before the crypto fund, he ran three: a $500 million mutual-fund intraday trading operation (Chronos, 1995-2004), a power forwards fund (Solios Power, 2004-2012), and a physically backed uranium trading fund. Uranium, power, crypto — he has always traded the hardest things to price.

In 2014 he founded Pythagoras in New York. The name, per the firm's own site, is a metaphor for arbitrage, market neutrality and pure mathematics. The fund does the most "boring" and steadiest work in crypto: cross-exchange arbitrage, funding-rate arbitrage, DeFi arbitrage. By 2021 it was among the first crypto hedge funds to cross $100 million in AUM.

Its finest hour came in 2022. FTX collapsed, Bitcoin fell roughly 60%, and the whole industry was bleeding. Yet Pythagoras's market-neutral arbitrage fund and trend-following fund both closed the year at around 8% — despite having 10% of the arbitrage fund's assets on FTX at the time. How? Per CoinDesk's reporting at the time, the team applied for a full withdrawal the moment FTX froze redemptions, recovered 7%, and hedged back the remaining 3% by shorting the FTT token and capturing the 3-5% discount between CME's bitcoin and ether futures and Binance pricing. The cost: redemptions cut AUM below $40 million.

Now he has chosen to step away. But the way he is stepping away looks different from most founders in this industry.

He redeems all of his own GP capital while keeping his profit interest and an advisory role. The successors are two people he hired straight out of graduate school — Eric Zhao (former COO and PM) becomes CEO, and Yusen Chau continues as CIO. Both joined at 24, and Dong now calls them the people who "can take Pythagoras to its next $1 billion in AUM." The new GP stake is backfilled by Zhao, Chau, most of the staff, and a group of long-term LPs, at a scale comparable to before the transition.

"I wouldn't leave unless I was sure my child, Pythagoras, was in the right hands," he wrote in the letter.

What's more worth talking about is what he is going to do. He is taking what he learned inside crypto exchanges back to the industry he started in — building an "electricity perpetual futures" exchange, so that AI data centers and AI agents can trade power automatically.

There is a real pain point here. AI data-center electricity demand is nothing like a traditional factory's: peak draw per rack is projected to equal the consumption of 65 households by 2027, and loads can swing more than 50% within seconds. Spot and forward power markets are not yet equipped to price and hedge demand that fast and that jumpy. Dong's idea is direct: take the crypto perp mechanism — no expiry, continuous trading — and bolt it onto electricity.

Pipe dream? Hard to say. But a person who has spent 12 years in crypto and half a lifetime in energy speaks both languages fluently. That combination is scarce in itself.

For quant practitioners, the real signal in this story is not "someone left a job." It's two things. First, the most mature infrastructure talent in crypto is migrating toward AI infrastructure — exchange mechanics, market making, settlement, the capabilities crypto ground out over a decade, now being carried over to harder, scarcer assets like power and compute. Second, founder succession at a crypto hedge fund has, for the first time, been done like a template: not emergency firefighting, but every due-diligence question answered in advance, in writing.

Like most of its crypto peers, the fund is not large, and AUM remains well below its peak. But the story makes one point: in a sector written off again and again, anyone who survives twelve years and hands over the baton alive is already rare.

Is your firm "changing people" or "adding people" this year? Those two signals tend to point toward very different next chapters.
