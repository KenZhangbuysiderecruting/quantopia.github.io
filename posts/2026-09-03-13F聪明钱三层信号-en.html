Tiger Global Cuts Alphabet 45% While Marshall Wace Piles Into IVV: Three Layers of Smart-Money Signal in Q2 13F Filings

2026-09-03 · Ken Zhang / Gravitas Recruitment

By mid-August, the Q2 13F filings were fully out. When $2.46 trillion of hedge fund long exposure is laid out across SEC forms, it tells you more than any quarterly letter.

Layer One: Trimming Mag-7 Is Consensus — But the Consensus Is Splitting

The headline number: 44% of filers trimmed the Magnificent Seven in Q2. But headlines hide what matters more — 56% were still adding or holding. This is not a one-way exit; it is "opinions changed, but in different directions."

Look at the specifics. Chase Coleman's Tiger Global cut Alphabet 45.4% — from 10.63 million shares to 5.81 million, roughly $1.72 billion sold, its largest sale of the quarter. It also cut Broadcom 51%, exited Netflix, and trimmed TSMC 12.3%. Yet Nvidia was down only 6.8% and Amazon just 3.2%.

What does this tell you? Coleman isn't fleeing tech — he is ranking inside the Mag-7. Ad-driven internet names (Alphabet, Meta) move down the priority list; AI infrastructure hardware (Nvidia, TSMC — still his top holding despite the trim) stays.

The buy side is more interesting. In Q2 Tiger Global opened new positions in Cerebras (3 million shares, ~$660M), AMD (675,000 shares, ~$392M), Seagate (~$275M) and Visa (~$274M), while increasing Intel by 159.5%.

Every one of its top ten holdings was reduced — yet the new positions cluster in AI chips, storage, and semi equipment. This is not "leaving AI." It is rotation within the AI trade: out of the application-layer leaders that have already run, into the next wave of beneficiaries across the infrastructure chain.

Layer Two: Marshall Wace Puts 23.6% of Its Filings Into IVV — an Active Quant "Indexing Itself"

Marshall Wace's Q2 13F portfolio was worth $126.7 billion, up 26% from $100.4 billion in Q1. Its single largest holding: the iShares Core S&P 500 ETF (IVV) at $29.8 billion — 23.6% of the entire disclosed book.

Paul Marshall's firm is known for active quant and long/short equity — not passive index tracking. When an active quant parks nearly a quarter of its disclosed book in a broad ETF, that signal is worth more than any single-stock move.

My read has two layers. First, risk management escalation: top-10 concentration in the S&P 500 has reached a record 37.5%, and taking beta through an ETF is cleaner than stock-picking it, cutting single-name tail risk. Second, capacity management: Marshall Wace reportedly planned to return ~$3.1 billion and trim total AUM toward ~$75 billion earlier this year, yet its Q2 US listed book (13F basis) expanded to $126.7 billion — meaning US equity exposure is growing, and ETFs are a far more efficient way to dial position sizes quickly than individual companies.

Paul Tudor Jones went the same contrarian way — Tudor Investment Corp raised its IVV stake roughly 730%. Two veteran macro traders simultaneously betting on the S&P 500 ETF is not a coincidence.

Layer Three: Berkshire Buys $37.7B of Alphabet While Bridgewater Cuts Nvidia and Alphabet

The real value of 13Fs has never been "who bought what." It is when smart money heads in opposite directions on the same name.

Berkshire Hathaway opened a new Alphabet position (Class A + C combined) worth roughly $37.7 billion in Q2 — the last disclosed portfolio adjustment before Warren Buffett steps down, and its significance is underestimated. Berkshire also kept trimming Apple.

But Bridgewater — Ray Dalio's fund — cut its Nvidia stake by nearly two-thirds and Alphabet by more than 50% in Q2. Coatue also trimmed Nvidia 14.1%.

Same quarter, same stock: Berkshire buying while Bridgewater sells. What does that say? That "AI valuation" has no consensus at the very top. Some are looking at 3–5 year discounted cash flows; others are watching quarterly momentum and concentration risk.

What This Means for Practitioners

If you are a quant researcher or PM, a 13F is not a trading signal (it lags 45 days) — it is an org chart of smart money. A few takeaways:

First, the AI trade has entered an "internal rotation" phase, not a full retreat. Second-line AI infrastructure names — Cerebras, AMD, Intel, Seagate — are being repriced by large growth funds. For systematic stock pickers, factor exposure needs rebalancing: pure AI momentum may have peaked, but the hardware supply chain of the AI capex cycle still has alpha.

Second, active funds using ETFs for beta management will become more common. Marshall Wace's 23.6% IVV position is an extreme case, but the direction is clear: when single-name concentration risk rises, an ETF sleeve is a cleaner way to take beta than individual stocks. For quants in portfolio construction, it is a reminder that hybrid "active + passive" strategies may become more mainstream than pure stock picking.

Third, read 13Fs against the seasonality of the talent market. Filings land in mid-August, right around the vesting windows of many HF professionals. The rhythm of smart-money repositioning and talent mobility is seasonally linked — Q2 rebalancing, Q3 performance, Q4 bonus negotiations. One alternative way to track 13Fs is as a leading indicator for hiring cycles.
