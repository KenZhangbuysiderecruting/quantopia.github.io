$3.7 Billion and 1.63 Seconds: How Deep Is the “Mechanical Business” of Index Rebalancing?

2026-08-20 · Ken Zhang / Gravitas Recruitment · Style B Industry Observer · P3 Veteran × F6 Comparison

1.63 seconds, $334 billion. That’s the volume matched in Nasdaq’s closing auction on June 26, 2026, the day of the Russell Index annual reconstitution. Across the whole market (Nasdaq + NYSE), $553.9 billion traded that day — $334 billion on the Nasdaq side and $219.9 billion on the NYSE. On the same day last year, the Nasdaq figure was $102.5 billion. A more-than-threefold jump, behind which sits a swelling passive capital behemoth — and an arbitrage ecosystem rotating around it.

The money isn’t from stock picking — it’s from the certainty that someone “must trade”

Put simply, index rebalancing earns the money that passive capital “has to trade.”

Market-cap-weighted indices (S&P 500, Nasdaq-100, Russell) must rebalance quarterly or semi-annually — when prices rise, weights drift up; when they fall, weights drift down, so ETFs and index funds must sell the risers and buy the fallers to pull weights back to target. Essentially mechanical mean reversion.

Equal-weight indices (like RSP) are even more aggressive, forcing all constituents back to equal weight every quarter. Turnover is higher, but structurally it’s a systematic “buy low, sell high.”

Two Millennium trading teams focused on this strategy — Glen Scheinberg and Pratik Madhvani — together made roughly $3.7 billion in June this year, more than half of the fund’s pre-fee profit for the month (about $6.6 billion). Millennium returned 4.1% in June and 10.5% year-to-date.

But don’t get envious just yet. In March 2025, the same people lost about $900 million when market turmoil from Trump tariffs hit. Millennium fell 1.3% in February and 1.2% in March, a cumulative drawdown of 2% for the first quarter.

This strategy isn’t explosive every year.

June this year: five things collided into a “perfect storm”

Why was June so extreme? Because five things stacked up in one month:

The S&P 500 quarterly rebalance. The Nasdaq-100 quarterly adjustment. The Russell annual reconstitution. SpaceX’s fast-track inclusion across multiple indices. Quarter-end multi-asset rebalancing.

SpaceX listed on June 12, the largest IPO in history. Both Russell and Nasdaq changed their rules to allow large new listings to be fast-tracked into indices just five trading days after listing. On the Nasdaq-100 side alone, JPMorgan estimated about $4 billion of passive buying; the Russell 1000 inclusion took effect the same month, with the buy volume undisclosed. The S&P 500, by contrast, held firm — it requires four consecutive quarters of GAAP profitability and a wait of at least 12 months after an IPO before consideration; SpaceX lost $4.9 billion in 2025 and doesn’t qualify.

Passive capital was forced to buy a loss-making company. Whatever ETFs you hold, whether you like it or not.

Equal-weight rebalancing is also reaping benefits in 2026. As of August 19, RSP (equal-weight S&P 500) had a year-to-date total return of roughly 15.7%, versus about 13.2% for SPY. In the narrow-momentum market of 2023-2024, equal weight got ground into the dirt. Put simply, equal-weight rebalancing isn’t “always winning” — it’s a structural bet that wins in breadth markets and loses in narrow-momentum markets.

Over a 10-year horizon, SPY returned 251% vs. RSP’s 207%. Same index, different bets.

The real risk hides where the money is best

The most valuable part of this strategy isn’t how much it can make — it’s its flaws.

Crowding. Millennium’s own people admit it’s “conceptually simple, but very few teams do it well.” The more transparent the index, the easier it is to calculate — and the sooner it gets priced in. The Financial Times counted roughly $1.3 trillion of index-rebalancing-related trading volume in Q2 2026, about twice the quarterly average of the past year and a record. When there are too many players, alpha decays.

Mechanically buying high and selling low. Rob Arnott’s research (covering S&P 500 constituent changes from 1989-2021) found that in the year after an addition becomes effective, deleted stocks outperform newly added ones by about 22% on average. Index funds are, essentially, buying at high prices and selling at low ones. Academic research estimates that moving rebalancing trades 3-12 months earlier or later could save about 12-23 basis points of hidden cost per year.

The rules aren’t purely mechanical. The S&P 500 committee has discretion — SpaceX was shut out of the S&P 500 but made it into the Russell and the Nasdaq-100. Pure rule-based models face “committee reversal” risk.

Slippage. On small-cap (Russell 2000) rebalancing days, an individual stock’s closing-auction volume can exceed several days of normal volume combined. In March 2020 during the pandemic, index providers simply canceled the rebalance, and funds that had bet on effective-date liquidity were caught flat-footed.

Headhunter view: where the bar is in this lane

From our perspective as quant headhunters, the hiring profile for index rebalancing strategies is unusual. It doesn’t require stock picking, but it does require mastery of index construction rules, the ability to precisely calculate passive fund flows, and a willingness to use leverage.

Very few teams can do it well. The people in these two Millennium pods largely came from legacy rebalancing teams at firms like Balyasny and D.E. Shaw’s Oculus. The candidate pool is extremely narrow, and the premium is high.

In short: this is a business that “looks simple but is extremely hard to do.” $3.7 billion sounds scary, but don’t forget it lost $900 million the year before.
