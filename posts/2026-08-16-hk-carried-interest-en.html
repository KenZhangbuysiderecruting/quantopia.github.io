DLD Expands to Hong Kong, Graham Capital Opens Its First Asia Office: How 0% Carried Interest Is Redrawing Asia’s Hedge Fund Talent Map

2026-08-16 · Ken Zhang / Gravitas Recruitment

Let’s start with a detail most people missed.

In the first week of August, three hedge funds with completely different styles planted their flags in Hong Kong almost simultaneously.

DLD Asset Management — the New York fund founded by Mark Friedman that manages nearly $3 billion in regulatory assets — moved its Asia-Pacific trading desk to Hong Kong and hired Maso Capital co-founder Sohit Khurana to lead it. (HedgeWeek, 2026-08-07)

Note that DLD isn’t equity long/short. It does relative value, arbitrage and event-driven; Friedman himself traded Asian convertible bonds at SBC Warburg and Deutsche Bank early in his career, and DLD’s DNA is really credits and arbitrage.

Xantium Group — the quant platform spun out of Tudor Investment Corp. — hired former Goldman Sachs partner Christina Ma to build its Asia business out of Hong Kong. (Wen Wei Po, 2026-08-11)

Graham Capital Management — a legacy macro/CTA fund managing about $21 billion — also opened its first Asian office in Hong Kong, to serve institutions and family offices across Hong Kong, Singapore, Japan and Korea. (Company website / Wen Wei Po, 2026-08-07/11)

Three people, three strategies, one city. That doesn’t look like coincidence.

The tax reform didn’t start on August 12, but August 12 drew a line

Many people focus on the government’s August 12 clarification. In fact, the bill itself was gazetted on June 12 — the Inland Revenue (Amendment) (Tax Concessions for Funds, Family-Owned Investment Holding Vehicles and Carried Interest) Bill 2026, whose core is expanding the carried interest concessionary tax regime and, if passed, taking effect retroactively from April 1, 2025.

The key point of the Financial Services and the Treasury Bureau’s August 12 response: proprietary trading firms don’t meet the definition of a “fund” and can’t get the 0% concession. Institutions that trade with their own capital — Jane Street, Citadel Securities, Jump Trading — are explicitly shut out.

That’s the Hong Kong government saying publicly: we want fund managers and family offices, not prop traders.

0% vs 24% — the math is very direct

For fund managers, carried interest is taxed at an effective rate of 0% in Hong Kong; in Singapore, individual performance fee income is taxed at the top personal income tax rate of 24%.

Sounds abstract? Assume a fund returns 15% annually and the manager takes a 20% performance fee — on $10 million of performance allocation, the after-tax difference is $2.4 million. For a small team, that’s already enough reason to move.

Hong Kong isn’t recruiting newcomers — it’s calling back old hands

Over the past five years, the direction of Asia’s hedge fund talent flows has been clear: Singapore.

Family office tax incentives, MAS’s regulatory framework, and relatively manageable living costs have led many fund managers and quant teams to move south.

But the tide is turning.

FengHe Fund Management — an Asian hedge fund managing about $9 billion and headquartered in Singapore — plans to open a Hong Kong office in H2 2026 and has already leased space in IFC. (Reuters/HedgeWeek, 2026-03/08)

This isn’t simply “opening one more office.” FengHe’s move back shows that when the tax gap gets large enough, geographical inertia breaks.

The latest data from Hong Kong’s SFC also supports this: as of end-2025, Hong Kong’s assets and wealth management business hit a record HK$42.2 trillion (about $5.4 trillion), up 20% year on year, with net fund inflows surging 193%. (SFC, 2025 Assets and Wealth Management Activities Survey, 2026-07-02)

Singapore won’t sit and take it

Of course, Singapore isn’t without a response.

The MAS is already discussing how to adjust its concessionary tax regimes to retain family offices and hedge fund talent. But policy adjustments run on cycles; Hong Kong’s 0% is in black and white, while Singapore’s response is still on the discussion table.

The window in between may be the key to Asia’s hedge fund talent reshuffling over the next 18-24 months.

Headhunter view: who moves first?

As a headhunter who’s been in this industry for over a decade, I see three types of people most likely to move first.

The first: arbitrage/relative-value PMs and small event-driven teams. Carried interest makes up a high share of their income structure, and the tax gap directly hits their take-home pay. Moves like DLD’s are essentially setting an example for these people.

The second: macro and CTA fund managers. Graham Capital’s entry into Hong Kong shows that even macro funds traditionally centered on New York/London have begun to treat Asia-Pacific as a market they must bet on.

The third: heads of Asia at platform funds. Pod shops like Xantium, Millennium and Balyasny hired a lot of people in Singapore over the past few years; now they have to reassess their footprint in Hong Kong — because competitors may already be there first.

But Hong Kong isn’t without costs either

High living costs, expensive office rents, a deeper but more fluid talent pool than Singapore — none of these old problems have gone away.

And the 0% carried interest solves only one thing: tax. It can’t replace regulatory clarity, can’t replace trading infrastructure, and can’t replace fund managers’ trust in the local market.

So my read is: this isn’t Hong Kong “winning” over Singapore — it’s Asia’s hedge fund talent map swinging from “unipolar” back to “bipolar competition.”

For practitioners, options have multiplied. For the hiring market, the game has become more complex.
