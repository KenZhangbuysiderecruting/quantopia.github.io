Tiansuan's RMB 10,000 Fine: The Same Clause Cost Another Party RMB 70,000

2026-11-12 · Ken Zhang / Gravitas Recruitment

On 3 November, the Shanghai Futures Exchange published two disciplinary decisions on the same day. Decision 249: Tiansuan Quant's "Index-Enhanced Protection Series 2" fund and one of its trading-desk employees, fined RMB 10,000. Decision 250: another individual, fined RMB 70,000 and barred from opening positions for twelve months.

Both cite the same clause — Article 32, paragraph 1, item 3 — and both describe the same conduct: reciprocal trading. One happened in silver options, the other in tin, nickel and alumina futures.

A sixfold difference. And the money is not the interesting part.

How wide the clause leaves the door

Article 32, paragraph 1, item 3 sets a range: order the party to rectify and confiscate any improper gains, then apply a warning, circulated criticism or public censure depending on severity; where there were no improper gains, or gains below RMB 100,000, a fine of up to RMB 500,000 may be added; where gains exceeded RMB 100,000, the fine runs from one to five times the gains.

Half a million is the ceiling. Ten thousand sits at 2% of it.

Another way to read it: the trade in question was worth RMB 50,580, and the fine was RMB 10,000 — 19.8% of the notional. Absurdly small in absolute terms, large enough as a ratio to make a headline. That is exactly where "penalty proportionate to the offence" gets hard.

Securities Times counted more than nine public reciprocal-trading decisions at the exchange this year, concentrated in illiquid options and far-month contracts: silver, gold, copper, aluminium, bitumen. The parties run from retail accounts and industrial traders all the way to the product accounts of multi-billion-fund managers.

The yardstick is not whether anyone made money. No gain, limited impact: circulated criticism plus a fine. Funds moved: a two-to-twelve-month ban on opening positions. Long-running violations across several contracts: outright market exclusion.

Decision 250 ran nearly four months, across several contracts, with money moving between a personal account and someone else's. It bought RMB 70,000, a public censure and a one-year ban.

Decision 249 was one trading day, one contract, one fill.

Whether you can still open positions is the expensive tier on this ladder.

One lot, two minutes

Tiansuan's account, as reported by Yicai and Lanjinger: on 12 February the firm was running a connectivity test on the Series 2 fund account, an operational error occurred, and one fill was formed against a non-fund account in the same contract. Exactly one fill, one lot on one side, RMB 50,580 notional, spotted and closed out within two minutes, reported internally the same day.

The firm drew two lines of its own: the employee works on the trading desk, executes orders, is not the fund's manager and does not run its investments; he has been removed from trading execution and disciplined internally. A self-review of every fund under management found nothing similar.

The industry calls a fill where two accounts held by the same people face each other "dui qiao" — matched trades. The firm denies the label, arguing the conditions are not met: no intent, a single fill, closed within two minutes and self-reported.

There is no need to guess at motive. The structure is enough: one person could move both his own account and a fund account, and was doing so while running a systems test.

That is not a question of character. It is a question of where authority stops.

Three desks, one line

Split the episode by job title and it becomes clearer.

Trading execution takes the order and works it. It does not touch strategy or sizing. Tiansuan's statement draws that line very cleanly — "responsible for the fund's specific trade execution", "does not manage the product's investments". That sentence is both an explanation and a defence.

Risk controls three things: limits beforehand, abnormal-trading surveillance during, review afterwards. If a test order reaches the exchange as a live order, where is the interception point?

Compliance owns reporting and verification: algorithmic-trading filings, account identity and suitability, replies to exchange enquiries.

One trader covering a dozen funds is normal in this industry. Add products without adding people and the separation between test and production environments rests entirely on process — and process rests on memory.

The incident happened in February; the notice came in November. Nine months of internal review, suspension and exchange deliberation. For the individual: RMB 10,000 and an entry in the integrity database. For the firm: an entry of its own, plus the line at the end of the notice telling the relevant futures brokerages to strengthen compliance education for clients and monitoring of online trading.

The rule that takes effect in four days says the same thing

The Measures for the Supervision and Administration of Derivatives Trading (Trial), CSRC Order No. 234, published on 14 May and effective 16 November — the first departmental rule for the derivatives industry under CSRC supervision.

Three provisions sit directly on today's line: real-name accounts plus investor suitability; derivative dealers must handle derivatives business separately from other business that presents conflicts of interest, with effective information barriers; and a full risk-management system, daily mark-to-market on margin, and documented pricing and valuation methodology, models and process.

"Handle separately, isolate effectively" translates into job language as: who may touch which account, who signs, who countersigns — written into policy, not left to habit.

Two hard thresholds as well: securities and futures firms applying for derivatives business need net capital above RMB 500 million for the preceding six months; the executive in charge needs at least three years of experience. From the effective date, firms that do not comply may not add business, and existing positions must run off at maturity.

The futures side is moving too. On 12 October, five exchanges jointly consulted on amendments to their disciplinary rules, closing on 19 October, adding six categories of algorithmic-trading violations with fines or punitive damages capped at RMB 500,000. Those amendments are still at the consultation stage and not in force — but the direction is clear: algorithmic trading has moved from "file a report" to "violations are punishable".

What it looks like on a job description

Trade support: watch the fills, watch the reconciliations, watch the odd order, and be able to explain how the test and production environments are governed.

Surveillance: not VaR-style risk, but the order behaviour itself — cancel ratios, fill share, matched trades between accounts under the same control.

Compliance, algorithmic-trading side: filings, client verification, answering exchange enquiries. The requirement has shifted over the past two years from "familiar with the regulatory framework" to "familiar with the specific articles of the exchanges' disciplinary rules".

Boundaries between these three blur most easily during expansion. Going from RMB 5 billion to 10 billion in assets doubles the trade count; the first headcount to get compressed is rarely research — it is these three desks.

Tiansuan's own trajectory fits: it crossed RMB 10 billion for the first time in May and has since slipped back into the 5-to-10 billion band; 272 funds are on file, 54 filed in 2025 — a record year — and four in 2026, with seven consecutive months of no new filings through November.

Assets rising fast and products launching quickly raises a practical question about whether the back office keeps pace. Hiring after the notice arrives means paying in explanations.

If you work at a quant firm, the datapoint worth reporting is headcount: how many people are dedicated to trade execution, to risk, and to compliance, against how many products you run.

Next: on 16 November the derivatives measures take effect. I will walk its three isolation requirements against actual job descriptions — which one genuinely adds a head, and which one just changes who signs.
