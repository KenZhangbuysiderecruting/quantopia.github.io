$150M vs $1B: SEC Pushes Hedge Fund Disclosure to 2027 — Half of Filers May Drop Out

2026-09-13 · Ken Zhang / Gravitas Recruitment

On September 3, the SEC and CFTC pushed the Form PF compliance deadline from October 1, 2026 to July 1, 2027. It is the fourth delay since the 2024 amendments. More interesting than the date is the threshold change proposed alongside it.

The full record of four delays

Form PF is a private-fund reporting form created after Dodd-Frank, shared by the SEC and CFTC, designed to give the FSOC visibility into leverage, exposures and liquidity across private funds.

On February 8, 2024, the two regulators adopted amendments that substantially expanded the reporting content, originally effective March 12, 2025. Then the delays began:

  • First: January 29, 2025 → moved to June 12, 2025.
  • Second: June 11, 2025 → moved to October 1, 2025.
  • Third: September 17, 2025 → moved to October 1, 2026.
  • Fourth: September 3, 2026 → moved to July 1, 2027.

From the original effective date, that is roughly 28 months of cumulative delay. The final rule took effect September 3, 2026 — Federal Register 91 FR 56593, with CFTC news release 9290-26 published in parallel.

The real change is in the thresholds

If this were only a delay, it would be a timing question. What changes the game is the proposal of April 20, 2026 (SEC Release IA-6959):

  • General reporting threshold: $150 million → $1 billion
  • Large hedge fund adviser threshold: $1.5 billion → $10 billion

By the SEC's own description, if the proposal is adopted, close to half of current filers would no longer need to file, and about two-thirds of large hedge fund advisers could exit quarterly and event reporting.

That is a two-orders-of-magnitude adjustment. $150 million to $1 billion removes a cohort of mid-sized firms from the reporting universe; $1.5 billion to $10 billion redraws what "large" means.

To be clear: the threshold change remains a proposal. What took effect this time is the delay, not the thresholds.

The official rationale

SEC Chairman Paul S. Atkins called the delay "practical and necessary."

The economic analysis in the Federal Register is blunt: the delay means the FSOC will receive expanded Form PF data later. The regulator wrote the cost into the rule text — acknowledging the data arrives late, but judging that industry readiness is the more pressing problem.

China is moving the other way

Over the same stretch, China has moved in the opposite direction.

The Measures for the Supervision and Administration of Information Disclosure of Private Investment Funds took effect on September 1, 2026, tightening disclosure requirements and giving custodians clearer reporting duties. On September 4, AMAC published a notice naming nine private fund managers it could not reach. Also on September 4, the CSRC opened consultation on the Measures for the Supervision and Administration of Private Investment Fund Fundraising.

One side raises the threshold to $1 billion and lets nearly half of filers exit; the other refines disclosure requirements and strengthens custodian responsibility.

This is not a question of who is right. The two markets are at different stages. The US private fund industry is large with mature data infrastructure, so the regulatory debate is about which data is actually useful. China's private fund industry is in a consolidation phase, where the priority is building the basic information channels.

The cost is written into coverage

Transparency has a price. The expanded Form PF asks for more granular position, leverage and liquidity data, which requires compliance and data systems that smaller firms must fund. Raising the threshold to $1 billion essentially lifts that cost off mid-sized firms.

The trade-off is coverage. The regulatory view narrows — only the largest firms remain visible. And risk accumulation has, more than once in history, started outside the visible field.

How that trade-off finally lands depends on the final version of the proposal. A question worth asking: is a $1 billion threshold sensible burden relief, or a blind spot?

One line to close

Four delays, 28 months, thresholds raised nearly sevenfold. Together they say one thing: US regulators are recalibrating how much to see and who has to report it.

Since February 2024, the expanded Form PF has never actually taken effect. Whether July 1, 2027 is the last date on the list, nobody knows.
