China's 10-Billion-RMB Quant Funds: The 90s Generation Takes the Helm

2026-09-27 · Ken Zhang / Gravitas Recruitment

Simuwang, a Chinese private-fund data provider, published a tally on September 24 that slices the 74 fund managers running 10bn+ RMB quantitative strategies by generation. The result is blunt: 42 were born in the 1980s — comfortably the majority and the backbone of the industry — and three managers born in the 1990s now sit in their firms' core governance seats, formally at the helm.

The number itself is small; the direction is what matters. The 10bn-RMB tier is the highest bar in Chinese quant — at that scale, strategies, systems and teams have all been tested by live trading. The archetype at this level used to be uniform: veterans with fifteen-odd years of investing behind them, leaving brokerages or overseas institutions to found their own shops. Now the 1990s cohort is appearing on this list — and holding the top seat.

The Structure

Of the 74 managers, 42 were born in the 1980s. Among the 29 of them with disclosed track records this year and over the past two years, 17 are simultaneously the actual controllers of their own firms — meaning most of the 10bn tier is already run by this generation, in firms they founded. The 1990s sample is still small: an August tally of 546 quant managers with disclosed performance counted just 23 born in the 1990s. By September's cut of the 10bn tier, three already hold governance seats. Smallest base, fastest climb.

The Paths

What's more informative than the numbers is the route. Not one of the newcomers took the traditional "grind your way up as a brokerage researcher" path.

Xue Yuxin of Huanian Private Fund came through USTC's gifted youth program, earned a physics PhD at the University of Tokyo, spent eight years in quant, and ran the equity alpha strategy at a leading 10bn fund before taking the helm. Jiang Linhao of Beijing Zhengding is the more archetypal case: a Tsinghua double degree in computer science and economics, a national informatics olympiad silver medal and an APIO gold medal while still a student, then a self-run trading team assembled from his Tsinghua competition circle in 2019, formal company founding in 2022, and assets above 10 billion RMB today.

Set the 1980s cohort beside them: Wang Yiping of Evolution Asset has 19 years of investing experience and founded his firm in 2014; Li Xiang of Mengxi Capital has 18 years of trading, from a low-latency background. The contrast is clear — the older generation served a ten-year apprenticeship before buying a ticket to the 10bn club; the younger generation runs a straight line from competitions and PhDs to core strategy seats. The detour through someone else's industry has been cut out entirely.

What It Means for Hiring

When I want to read how an industry's hiring logic is shifting, I look at who now sits at the top. This baton pass changes at least three things.

Interview depth is changing. The people now setting the bar were themselves selected by gifted programs and competition medals; the yardstick they apply to interns is "how would you verify this factor isn't overfitting", not "where did you intern". Titles are depreciating; verifiable research output is appreciating.

Team shapes are changing too. The new generation are mostly strategy leads turned managers, and they build teams like research task forces: small, full-cycle, everyone close to live trading. The old division — researchers mine factors, someone else ships them — will get rarer in these shops.

And the pay conversation is changing. These bosses were themselves bid for by the market; they know their own price and roughly know yours. The information gap is closing, and what's left to negotiate is substantive: strategy direction, data access, co-investment mechanics.

If you were choosing between two 10bn funds — one founded by a 19-year veteran, one helmed by a 90s-generation manager — which way would you lean? There's no standard answer. The steady hand and the fast hand each have their case. But a generational baton pass, once begun, doesn't run backwards: the next five years will very likely hold more than three 90s-generation names on this list. For candidates, the more useful question isn't which generation runs the desk — it's which research process you want to work in.

What year was your head of research born? The answer says more about a team than its AUM does.
