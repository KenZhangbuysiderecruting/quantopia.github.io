Goldman's 25% vs 11%: Hedge Funds Now Have More Money Than People

2026-09-20 · Ken Zhang / Gravitas Recruitment

Freddie Parker, who runs prime brokerage relationships at Goldman Sachs, said something on the AIMA podcast a few days ago: every time someone announces that multi-manager platforms have peaked, they go up another step.

It sounds like a polite line to say on a podcast. What he had in his hand was more interesting — a fresh set of numbers.

Goldman Prime's own data: over the past 12 months, the multi-strategy hedge funds it tracks grew assets by more than 25%, and the sector now runs well past $500bn. Over the same period, headcount at these platforms grew 10% to 11%.

Money grew more than twice as fast as people.

Two numbers worth putting side by side: assets +25%, headcount +10-11%. Multi-manager platforms are moving "growth" off the hiring spreadsheet and onto the allocation spreadsheet.

This is not a single data point. Citco, the largest fund administrator globally, published mid-September figures: in the first eight months of 2026, net inflows into the hedge funds it administers reached $99.7bn — already above the $62.2bn recorded for all of 2025. Multi-strategy funds took $57.1bn of that, close to 60%.

The flow is also highly concentrated. Funds above $10bn in AUM absorbed $77bn this year, more than three quarters of all inflows. Funds below $1bn picked up roughly $1bn in August alone.

AFI, the industry outlet, tracks twelve multi-manager platforms above $10bn. Their combined assets went from $373.2bn to $456.4bn in a year, up 22%. Schonfeld, Walleye, Point72 and Balyasny each grew assets more than 45%.

The reflex here is obvious: more money than people, so surely a hiring wave is coming.

Goldman reads it the other way. The gap between asset growth and headcount growth is largely filled by external allocations — platforms distribute capital to outside managers, get the capacity, and those managers never show up on their own payroll.

Parker did leave two caveats. March and July this year rank among the three worst months on record for multi-manager platforms (the other being March 2020). And money keeps coming in, so "there will be allocation pressure over the next 12 months, which will continue to push up the war for talent."

In plain terms: capital is not the constraint. People who can absorb it are.

Now pull the camera back to China, same autumn recruiting season, a different picture. On September 18, Shenzhen ran its first finance-sector campus recruitment fair under the "Jinyao Pengcheng" banner. Close to 80 financial institutions brought nearly 3,000 openings across quant, securities, futures and public funds, with top packages around RMB 500,000 a year. More than 4,000 candidates walked in; 65% held master's degrees.

Globally the story is "plenty of money, people used sparingly." Domestically it is "openings expanding, supply flooding." Two sides of the same coin.

From a headhunter's seat, one structural change sits inside these numbers: platforms buying capacity no longer means platforms buying headcount.

It breaks down into three layers.

Each PM now runs more money, so pricing for people who directly produce capacity keeps moving up. That is the layer Parker meant by the war for talent.

Hiring logic has shifted from expansion to replacement plus external allocation. Total headcount up 11% looks mild, but internal churn is accelerating: money grows fast, seats do not follow, and competition for PM seats gets harder, not easier.

The graduate door has not closed. Campus hiring is expanding in China, and internship programmes overseas keep running. But the value of that first seat increasingly depends on whether you join a growing platform or a small pool dividing a fixed pie — Citco's flow data has already ranked the answer for you.
