Fasanara Flips the Quant Playbook: Skip the Big PMs, Back the Small Teams

2026-08-23 · Ken Zhang / Gravitas Recruitment

Multi-strategy platforms are all racing to get bigger. Fasanara is going the other way — it has built a platform called Open Quant that funnels capital to quant teams that haven’t scaled yet. On August 12, it hired Dr. Darran Specter from Brevan Howard to hunt for these emerging equity quant teams globally.

The appointment itself is a signal about where capital is flowing.

Turning an allocator from ADIA and Brevan Howard into a talent radar

Specter’s resume is rare in the quant industry. He isn’t a PM who came out of a pod — he spent more than 20 years as a buy-side allocator. He spent 11 years at Abu Dhabi Investment Authority (ADIA), allocating billions of dollars to systematic hedge funds; in 2024 he joined Brevan Howard as Senior Portfolio Strategist, building PM evaluation and capital allocation frameworks. He is now based in London as Fasanara’s Head of Quant Equity.

Fasanara isn’t asking him to run a strategy; it’s asking him to find a group of people: emerging external equity quant teams around the world.

This effectively internalizes the traditional institutional “fund selection” capability as the platform’s “production” capability.

Open Quant is not another pod shop

Many people’s first reaction: isn’t this just a smaller Millennium? No.

The traditional pod shop logic: we raise our own PMs, give them capital, risk and middle/back office, and the PMs run strategies on the platform. Open Quant’s logic: we don’t grow big PMs — we go out and find small teams that are already formed but haven’t yet secured institutional capital, give them money, give them infrastructure, and let them run on the platform.

Fasanara officially positions the platform as multi-manager and multi-strategy, currently allocating capital to roughly 30 independent managers globally, with strategies spanning arbitrage, volatility, equity market neutral, quant macro and tactical strategies. The underlying belief is straightforward: capacity-constrained early-stage managers tend to outperform those that have already scaled.

Is that judgment backed by data? Open Quant rose 1.5% in May this year, with a year-to-date return of 6.7%, and took in $52 million in May inflows — the platform’s best month ever. To be sure, it’s still small — Fasanara as a whole manages about $6 billion in assets, and the Quant platform is close to $150 million. But the growth rate matters more than the absolute size.

(Note: the May performance and inflow figures come from an Alternative Fund Insight report dated June 3, 2026; this is secondhand media reporting and the primary source was not disclosed.)

Why is this direction emerging now?

Two backdrops.

First, the top platforms are getting increasingly “heavy.” The platform models at Citadel, Millennium and Point72 have pushed the industry to very high barriers — infrastructure, compliance, capital and talent, all indispensable. New teams trying to raise independently face institutional investors’ due diligence hurdles and minimum allocation requirements.

Second, capital is concentrating, but alpha is dispersing. Large platforms can capture scalable, relatively transparent strategies; the truly niche, capacity-constrained, still-early signals are often hidden in small teams not yet covered by institutions. What Open Quant wants to do is systematically discover and support these teams.

Specter’s own words in the announcement: “I look forward to working with the team to continue developing the platform, deepening our manager network and identifying emerging quant talent globally.” That sounds official, but translated into headhunter language: Fasanara is preparing to turn “finding emerging quant teams” into a long-term business backed by process, technology and capital.

Implications for headhunters and talent

This signal is especially interesting for the Asian market.

Over the past two years, Hong Kong and Singapore have seen a flood of multi-strategy platform expansion, competing for mature PMs and researchers. But Fasanara’s model opens a new track: not mature PMs, but emerging teams that “have a strategy but lack capital and infrastructure.” For quant startup teams in Hong Kong and small groups in Singapore that recently left big platforms or funds, this is a new source of capital.

Over the longer term, allocators moving to the front end of platforms will reshape the demand structure for quant talent. In the future, platforms won’t just need researchers who can write alpha; they’ll need people who can evaluate alpha, build screening frameworks and do capital allocation. Career paths like Specter’s — from allocator to producer — are likely to become more common.
