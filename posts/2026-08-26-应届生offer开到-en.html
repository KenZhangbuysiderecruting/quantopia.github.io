New-Grad Offers Reaching $1.5 Million — While the Median Quant Researcher Earns Just $200K

2026-08-26 · Ken Zhang / Gravitas Recruitment

Let's start with a number that sounds like a joke: this year, some quant firms have been writing $1.5 million offers for entry-level talent. Not after a decade of work — in year one. Wall Street and Silicon Valley have been fighting over math geniuses to the point of absurdity. A recent Wall Street Journal story was headlined outright as "The Million-Dollar Battle for Math Talent." The core point is one sentence: trading firms and AI labs are fighting over the same people — the brightest math and CS students. Headhunters slice this into two eras: pre-OpenAI and post-OpenAI. Matt Stabile, founder of recruitment firm Stabile Search, put it this way: seven-figure offers used to be a rarity; now "no one blinks at a million dollars anymore."

How Extreme the Numbers Get at the Top

At top trading firms, a standard new-grad total package is $350K–$500K a year, and reaching seven figures after a few years is not rare.

Kevin Cassata, founder of Karbon Agency, a headhunter focused exclusively on quant executives, says he has personally seen $1.5 million offers extended to entry-level candidates. To lock in a summer intern who has not even graduated, some PMs are even willing to break their firm's existing comp framework.

Alvaro Cartea, director of the Oxford-Man Institute of Quantitative Finance, put it even more bluntly: his mathematics PhD students, on entering quant, earn $300K–$1M a year; summer internships at quant firms can pay over $30K a month.

But the Median Is Nothing Like That

This is the tension I most want to highlight. Those seven-figure numbers above are the "top," not the "norm."

Now look at something closer to the ground. Kadoa aggregated 769 quant job listings in August 2026. The median base salaries: $201K for quant researchers, $200K for quant developers, and $197K for quant traders.

Broken down by firm, the gap is starker. Jump Trading's median is $275K, Citadel's $268K, Two Sigma's $233K — that is the top tier. Below them: Millennium $205K, QRT $203K, Point72 $175K, Squarepoint $150K, and Man Group down at $108K.

Same label — "quant." A top entry offer of $1.5M vs. a median base of $150K at mid-tier firms. A tenfold gap.

(One clarification: $1.5M is total offer value, $150K is median base — the two are not fully comparable. But the direction is unmistakable: the money is heavily concentrated in a tiny group, not spread evenly across the industry.)

That Gap Is the Market Pricing Scarcity

In recruitment, I never look at averages when it comes to pay — I look at medians and tails.

Seven-figure offers sound scary, but they lock in a tiny minority: IMO and IOI medalists, PhDs who can write trading code and train large models at the same time, and seasoned hands moving with live signal track records. That pool numbers a few hundred people globally. When Wall Street and Silicon Valley fight over both ends of it, prices naturally get bid into the stratosphere.

The vast majority of quant researchers take home that ~$200K median. Nothing to sneeze at — but a world away from "a million dollars."

What is truly valuable has never been the phrase "knows quant" — it is the intersection skill scarce enough that two markets bid against each other for it. Wall Street wants people who can forecast markets; Silicon Valley wants people who can train models. When one person can do both, they become the shared prey of two sets of buyers. Cartea said it plainly: students with an LLM background get the top tier.

What Should Aspiring Entrants Listen To?

Two sentences — listen to both.

First: do not let seven figures brainwash you. You will most likely land at the median tier, so put your energy into "moving toward the tail" — get strong at both math and engineering, then add hands-on "research with AI" experience.

Second: do not forget the seven figures are real. A reminder for anyone still eyeing traditional bank quant roles: those Oxford PhDs — almost none of them want to interview at investment banks anymore. Cartea's relayed quote is blunt: "Among the people I know, I have not heard of anyone willing to accept a traditional investment bank offer."
