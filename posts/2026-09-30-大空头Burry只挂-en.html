Burry Lends Only His Name: Short-Only Funds Shrink from 54 to 6

2026-09-30 · Ken Zhang / Gravitas Recruitment

While Wall Street is still hunting for a bubble in AI stocks, the man who made his name shorting subprime in 2008 has turned to a quieter corner: private credit.

On September 28, Reuters reported that a new short-focused fund called Minerva Investment Management is about to launch, with founder Laks Ganapathi bringing in Michael Burry as a senior adviser. Burry needs little introduction — Michael Lewis's The Big Short was written about him, and Christian Bale played him on screen.

The interesting part is not what he is bearish on. It is that this time he is only lending his name, not running the trade himself.

Late last year Burry wound down his own Scion Asset Management and launched a paid Substack, Cassandra Unchained, which now has more than 300,000 subscribers. At $39 a month, the newsletter alone could gross in excess of $100 million a year at the ceiling. So why would a man who already earns from writing and trades his own money freely put his name behind a new fund nobody has heard of? Because the battlefield he picked is precisely where most people have already left.

The short-only fund is disappearing as a species. By HFR's estimate, funds focused on short strategies fell from 54 in 2008 to six in the second quarter of 2026. Eighteen years, nine-tenths gone. The reasons are not complicated: regulators watch short positions closely, shorting is chronically painful in a bull market, and US hedge fund disclosure rules have changed. The 2021 GameStop squeeze put the risk of crowded shorts on the table — when a price detaches from fundamentals, even a correct thesis gets run over.

So Minerva entering now is itself a contrarian signal. And what Burry is underwriting by lending his name is something more specific: private credit defaults.

Minerva is targeting companies in healthcare, retail, restaurants and small banks that are kept alive by private credit. Ganapathi's own words are that these companies are tied to private credit financing and may be "quietly rotting". Fitch data show the US private credit default rate reached 6.3% annualised in August, a record high. And the worst thing about private credit is its opacity — a bad loan can sit inside model valuations for years until a default, a bankruptcy or a forced refinancing blows it up.

Underneath is a story about talent. Shorting is a counter-instinctual craft: you have to hold your line when nobody believes you, carry mark-to-market drawdowns and redemption pressure, and wait for a turn that may never come. Burry is the living example — he shorted subprime from 2005 to 2007, lost money for two full years while investors demanded redemptions, and only then caught the turn in housing. Scion Capital returned 489% net from its 2000 founding through June 2008, against roughly 2% for the S&P 500. The longest part of that curve was the decline.

That is why short talent went from 54 firms to six — it is not that nobody knows how to short. It is that almost nobody can hold on.

As a recruiter, the signal I read is this: when the number of practitioners in a strategy shrinks to single digits, the handful at the top become extremely scarce. You can count on one hand the people still active in the market and still willing to run pure shorts. By using Burry's name as an endorsement, Minerva is essentially taking a nearly extinct capability and using it to pry open an asset class almost nobody dares to touch. Ganapathi admits her approach to Burry was personal — an introduction through a friend, then the discovery that Burry subscribed to her newsletter, which opened the door.

It also explains Burry's own shift: from running a fund himself to endorsing someone else's fund with his name, and monetising his judgement through a newsletter. For someone who has done this for more than two decades and deeply dislikes noise, it may be a lighter way to work than launching another fund.

So here is the question: in an era when pure short funds are nearly extinct, someone has walked back in under the "Big Short" banner. Is that an opportunity, or one more lonely bet?

Is your firm hiring people who can go long this year, or people who can carry a drawdown?
