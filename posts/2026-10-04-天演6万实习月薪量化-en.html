Tianyan's RMB 60,000 Intern Salary: China's Quant Campus Hiring Moves on Money and Bar

2026-10-04 · Ken Zhang / Gravitas Recruitment

Day two of China's National Day holiday, and the quant campus recruiting battlefield did not take the day off.

Two numbers first. One from Shanghai: Tianyan Capital is advertising a monthly salary for quantitative research interns from RMB 60,000, with performance bonuses uncapped. The other from overseas: Millennium has opened AI "digital twins" to all 7,000-plus employees, following 1,600 AI colleagues already on staff.

On one side money is being pushed to RMB 60,000; on the other, AI is being slid onto every desk. What has really changed in this campus season may not be the money. It is the yardstick.

Start with the money. A Securities Times report on October 1 laid out the autumn recruiting board: beyond Tianyan, Shanghai Heyi Investment advertised a "RMB 20,000 tech gift pack on day one" in its campus poster, internship day rates of RMB 800 to 1,500, plus staff housing; recruiting platforms listed a row of quant researcher roles at RMB 60,000-90,000 a month and 30 months' pay; and several private funds have extended their reach to the 2027 and 2028 graduating classes, with core roles starting at RMB 40,000 a month.

AI algorithm researcher and AI engineer are the most frequent phrases in this year's autumn job descriptions. JiuKun took its information sessions into Peking University, with roles running from quantitative strategy researcher through to AI engineer; Minghong and Kuande launched 2027 global campus campaigns in parallel. On university careers sites in Beijing and Shanghai, these posters are going up one after another.

But with money stacked this high, one detail deserves more attention.

In the same report, an HR manager at a Shanghai quant private fund said something blunt: after AI became ubiquitous, the overall coding ability of fresh graduates has actually declined — many students lean heavily on AI at university, and after they convert to full-time it shows.

The same week brought a cooling note: this year's AI talent market is calmer than last year's. Last year large-model fundraising was hot and a few AI companies paid astonishing sums; this year the sector has returned to reason, and candidates' income expectations have come back to normal ranges.

Money rising, the market cooling, the read on ability getting stricter. Three things at once point to one judgement: what firms fear is not failing to hire. It is hiring someone who can only direct AI and cannot catch the ball themselves.

That judgement has a more concrete footnote overseas.

Business Insider reported on September 29 that Millennium, with $97bn under management, has opened "digital twins" to all staff — each twin has its own mailbox, permissions and audit log, can only interact with its own assigned employee, and cannot make decisions for anyone. In an earlier pilot group of more than 150 people, 97% talked to their twin every day; now new joiners get one on day one. Last month the firm also announced a joint effort with Anthropic on an AI digital risk analyst, and this year's internship programme opened an AI track.

When this account wrote about overseas job descriptions last month, AI tooling was still a "nice to have" in a Citadel Securities role. A month later, the same thing has gone from a line in a JD to a colleague issued on day one.

Overseas, AI is being assigned to people already on payroll; in China, "can use AI" is being written into the campus bar. Both ends are doing the same thing: redefining what "the basics" means in this industry.

So one question is worth every person staring at autumn recruiting: where does your firm's JD put AI this year — nice to have, proficient, or expert?

"Proficient with AI" on a CV is already worth little. The interview pushes one level down: what have you built with AI? When was it wrong, and how did you catch it? Whoever can answer that is the person a firm will pay RMB 60,000 a month for.

One caveat: an HR manager's impression is not industry-wide data, and the firms willing to say this out loud carry their own screening anxiety.

For the record in the comments: nice to have, proficient, or expert?
