Goldman's $20B Sell Ticket: Hedge Funds' Worst Week in Two Years

2026-11-01 · Ken Zhang / Gravitas Recruitment

A 2% weekly loss — the worst showing for systematic long-short strategies since 2024. Zoom out, though, and the same cohort is still up 18.7% on the year.

Those figures come from a Goldman Sachs prime brokerage note dated October 26, seen in full by Reuters. The same note carried a starker line: over the past two weeks, hedge fund accounts clearing through Goldman net sold $20 billion of technology stocks — within arm's reach of July's $23 billion unwind.

Painful, but not bone-deep — that's the first reading. The more interesting one: what hurt this time is a different disease from July.

Start with the tape. Meta broke sentiment: down 10% on the week after it said it would issue a new class of shares to fund its AI capital spending. October 22 was worse — all three strategy buckets Goldman tracks (fundamental L/S, systematic, multi-strategy) fell more than 1% in a single day, the first time since the Covid crash. Tony Pasquariello, Goldman's head of hedge fund coverage, wrote that funds dumped tech stocks last Thursday at the fastest pace in two years.

Yet the S&P 500 fell just 0.46% that week.

Money didn't leave the market. It migrated out of the AI names hedge funds own most, into energy, small caps and value. The index didn't hurt; the positioning did — systematic and momentum strategies had stacked last year's chips on the same AI heavyweights.

The third hook is on the short side. Investing.com, citing the same Goldman PB ledger, reported that long sales outpaced short covering by about 3.4 to 1, and net leverage among fundamental L/S funds fell to 47% — a one-year low. In other words, last week's main source of losses wasn't a long-side stampede; it was crowded shorts getting squeezed while the broader market fell. Losing money when your shorts rally into a falling tape grinds harder than a one-way drawdown.

Now the July comparison. That round was momentum factors giving back gains, with deleveraging roughly 90% complete — normal pressure release after a big run. This round is different: net leverage is already at a one-year low and long books are thin, leaving little room to de-gross further. But something new hangs overhead — Goldman derivatives strategist Lee Coppersmith's estimate (a note restricted to professional clients; the figure is not independently verifiable) suggests CTAs may still have roughly $65 billion of US equities to sell if the S&P keeps sliding. July was the pressure release; this time the ammunition isn't spent.

I'd hold some humility: the PB data runs only through end-October. Multi-strategy funds lost 1.9% in October (JPMorgan's PB tally) while quant was only slightly negative. It will take another two to three weeks of prime brokerage data to tell whether this was a reset or the opening act of a broader de-risking. Nobody can call the year-end with conviction.
