100,000 Cores at Lingjun: The Card Tripled, and a New Department Appeared

2026-11-11 · Ken Zhang / Gravitas Recruitment

In early November, Caijing's Yuanchuan published a piece on quant funds moving into Ulanqab, and Lingjun reposted it on its own site. One set of numbers: Lingjun's cluster there runs more than 100,000 CPU cores, over a thousand GPUs and more than 10PB of storage, built since 2020. Same article, another set: an 8-card A100 server cost about RMB 400,000 last year and more than RMB 1.2 million now. Graphics cards tripled in a year.

How to spend the money is one layer. Below it is where the jobs go.

First, which machine room moves

Quant firms run two kinds of machine rooms, and people conflate them.

The trading room has to sit as close to the exchange as possible; a millisecond matters, so it stays in an office tower in Beijing, Shanghai or Shenzhen and nobody moves it. The training room is the opposite: large, long-running, insensitive to latency, and its only enemy is operating cost. Only the second kind ever went to the grassland.

Why Ulanqab? Three things.

Cold. Average annual temperature of 4.3°C, and for close to ten months a year outside air can cool the halls directly. Cooling normally eats about 30% of a large data centre's electricity; skip it and PUE drops toward 1.1.

Power. Delivered electricity holds at RMB 0.32–0.36 per kWh, against 0.6–0.8 for industrial users in Beijing and Shanghai. Half the price, multiplied by a cluster that never switches off, is a large number.

Distance. Just over 300 km from Beijing, with a dedicated fibre link and single-digit-millisecond latency. Far enough for cheap land and power, close enough to send someone for the day.

The card tripled, and the arithmetic changed

The most concrete line in that article: cards are up about threefold in a year, switches, CPUs and memory up about twofold, and many SKUs simply unavailable at any price.

Compute used to be rented on demand, booked as an expense, written off at year-end. With a threefold move in card prices, buying beats renting, and buying early beats buying late by the price of a car. Procurement moves from expense to asset, from one person's approval to a year-long capex plan.

A rough calculation at current quotes: Lingjun's thousand-odd GPUs, at RMB 1.2 million per 8-card server, is about 125 machines, or RMB 150 million — servers only, excluding networking, storage and the building. That is an estimate from public prices, not their cost. Firms that built before 2021 bought at a completely different structure; in hindsight their edge was one word: cheap.

One constraint is easy to miss, per a November report in Korea's Maeil Business Newspaper: data centres that have run for a year or more with rack occupancy below 50% do not get new permits. In management language — the cards must run full. Falling short does not just waste money, it blocks the next expansion. Effective utilisation has moved from a technical metric to a business one.

The machine room is on the grassland; the owner is in the city

One quiet line in the original piece: the algorithm teams at the big platforms, and the research teams at quant funds, mostly stayed in Beijing; what sits at the machine room is racking, power and operations.

That implies a new organisational cut. One firm split into two locations, two reporting lines, two sets of KPIs. On the grassland the scorecard is uptime, rack fill, PUE and delivery schedule; in the city it is factors, alpha and drawdown. A dedicated line and a scheduling spreadsheet connect the two.

Who owns the question of whether a given machine was fully used today? Five years ago the question did not exist, because compute was rented from a cloud provider and idle capacity was handed back. Self-built capacity cannot be handed back.

This now has a C-level title

In early November, Point72 appointed Faisal Shamsee as chief infrastructure engineering officer, based in Singapore. He spent 25 years at Goldman Sachs, started as a systems administrator, made partner in 2022, and left as co-head of Goldman's Singapore operations after leading core engineering for Asia Pacific.

He is not an outlier. Ilya Gaysinskiy, who joined Point72 as CTO in 2024, was also a Goldman partner; Rahul Garde now heads platform infrastructure; chief risk officer Michael Fisher is another Goldman alumnus. A hedge fund's technology stack recruiting repeatedly from one investment bank — and not for strategy seats.

The title is the statement. If infrastructure were a support function, an IT director would do. A dedicated chief officer means someone who signs, carries the risk, and negotiates as a peer with the head of research.

Three job descriptions, three prices

Three comparable postings went up on Chinese recruiting platforms in the past two months. Side by side, they are telling.

A quant firm in Shenzhen is hiring a cluster operations engineer at RMB 13–25k a month, one head, for job scheduling, monitoring and alerting, capacity planning and troubleshooting. Keep it up.

A quant firm in Shanghai wants a cluster systems engineer at RMB 25–45k with 15 months' pay, three heads, to build and maintain mixed CPU/GPU clusters, keep raising effective utilisation, and work on a petabyte-scale market-data storage engine. Make it faster.

A Shenzhen AI-infra role placed by a headhunter pays RMB 50–90k with 24 months' pay and is hiring ten people at once, for bare-metal Kubernetes, scheduling across ten-thousand-GPU clusters, and a distributed training platform. Build it from scratch.

Same "compute department", RMB 13k to 90k, a spread of more than five times. Not seniority — three different jobs. The ten-head posting is still being refreshed, and that density says more than the salary numbers do.

What cannot be bought

Cards can be bought, power can be negotiated, land can be acquired. All three have a price and a supplier.

What cannot be bought is the person who can read an InfiniBand topology, calculate the power density of a rack, argue a construction schedule with the contractor, negotiate tariffs with the local utility, and tell the head of research that this particular experiment is not worth three hundred cards.

These people are not in any ready-made talent pool. They sit inside cloud providers, supercomputing centres, chip vendors' field-application teams, and a handful of quant firms that built their own clusters early.

If you work at a quant firm, the useful datapoint is headcount: how many people in your team are dedicated to cluster and GPU scheduling, against total headcount.
