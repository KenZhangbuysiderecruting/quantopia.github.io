Citadel's 180-Person Quant Group: Is Writing Code Worth Less?

2026-10-18 · Ken Zhang / Gravitas Recruitment

Navneet Arora, who heads global quantitative strategies at Citadel, put it in one line worth copying onto any quant resume: humans are becoming "managers of machines". This is not a metaphor. Bloomberg reported on Sept 25 that Citadel's roughly 180-person quant group is expanding, with headcount set to grow at a "healthy" double-digit rate over the next year. The group is split between financial researchers and data engineers.

Two things that run against the obvious read

The first is sourcing. A hedge fund recruiting out of AI labs was not routine a few years ago. Citadel's competitor list now runs past banks, hedge funds and prop shops to include OpenAI and Anthropic. COO Gerald Beeson calls it "a new frontier" in recruiting.

The second matters more: as AI spreads, the premium on coding skills is falling. Arora's framing is that AI lowers the value of coding and raises the value of researchers who can generate investment ideas and steer machines into profitable trades. One person can now run two, four, sometimes more processes at once.

Read only the first half and it sounds like another "programmers are finished" headline. The second half is where the signal is — what gets repriced upward is not the act of using AI, but knowing what to ask it to compute. Once tools are commoditised, scarcity moves from execution to problem definition.

The number that explains the hiring

The same Bloomberg piece carries a figure more blunt than any argument. Recent New York University research suggests a profitable trading signal can lose half its excess return in about 18 months — down from five to seven years before AI was widely adopted.

Eighteen months against roughly 2,000 days. The half-life of a signal has compressed by more than seventy percent.

That explains two things. First, why firms pay up for people who can generate ideas: ideas themselves depreciate faster, so the only defence is faster, denser output. Second, why a quant group now carries so many data engineers: when signals die quickly, you have to keep re-mining new data, and data infrastructure stops being support and becomes the production line.

And the cost signal nobody quotes

Arora is explicit that technology and talent are both more expensive, that fixed costs have risen, and that scale therefore matters more than ever. Citadel's response is to keep going bigger — while requiring non-compete agreements of up to two years for investing staff.

Scale matters more, non-competes get longer, and talent comes from AI labs. Put those three together and an institution is admitting the ticket price of this competition keeps rising, and the number of players who can stay at the table keeps shrinking.

What it means if you are the candidate

First, the job map. Data engineers are now written into the description of a quant group alongside financial researchers, not as a supporting act. If you have an engineering or science background and want into quant, the researcher track is not the only door.

Second, the skill ordering. Writing Python is the entry ticket, not the edge. What sets your ceiling is whether, on seeing an anomaly in the data, you can say what it might be before you touch a keyboard.

AI made building things cheap. It made thinking clearly expensive.
