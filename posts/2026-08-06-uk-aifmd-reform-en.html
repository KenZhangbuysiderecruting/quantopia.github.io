Eight Years After Brexit, the UK Finally Shakes Off EU Hedge Fund Regulation: Three Tiers Replace One-Size-Fits-All, and Asian HFs’ London Offices Are Becoming the Biggest Winners

2026-08-06 · Ken Zhang / Gravitas Recruitment · Style B Industry Observer · P2 Critic × F1 News Commentary

On July 14, the UK Treasury (HMT) and the Financial Conduct Authority (FCA) quietly published a 36-page draft statutory instrument and a consultation paper.

Almost nobody in the Chinese-speaking world noticed. But this document — The Alternative Investment Fund Managers Regulations 2026 — may be one of the most consequential regulatory changes for the global hedge fund industry in 2026.

Its core logic is simple: eight years after Brexit, the UK has finally decided not to follow the EU’s AIFMD II, but to write its own new rules. And the direction of the new rules is — relaxation.

[Key Numbers at a Glance]

· Publication date: 2026/07/14 (HMT SI draft + FCA CP26/28 + CP26/26 + CP26/27)

· Three tiers (based on NAV): Small <£750m / Medium £750m-£5bn / Large >£5bn

· Consultation deadline: 2026/10/14; FRAME by 9/22, remuneration by 9/16

· Expected effective date: 2028 (rule-revocation-type changes may come earlier)

· Estimated annual compliance cost savings: £128m (FCA official figures)

· FRAME reporting burden target reduction: 75%

· Total size of the UK alternative asset management market: £2 trillion (world’s second largest)

01 | One-sentence summary: three tiers replace one-size-fits-all

For the past 13 years, UK hedge fund regulation has copied the EU’s AIFMD rules, drawing the line by AUM: AIFMs with “full-scope” status above €500 million had to do full compliance; below that, “sub-threshold” small funds got relief.

The problem: the rules hadn’t moved in 14 years. The €500 million threshold was never adjusted for inflation, with the result that more than half of UK hedge funds were stuck carrying full-scope compliance burdens even though their AUM never met what was once the “large” standard.

The new plan cuts the one-size-fits-all approach and replaces it with a three-tier framework based on NAV (net asset value):

· Small AIFMs (NAV <£750m) — basic valuation policy plus risk monitoring, with almost no leverage restrictions;

· Medium AIFMs (NAV £750m-£5bn) — independent risk management function plus detailed valuation, but much lighter than today’s full-scope;

· Large AIFMs (NAV >£5bn) — retain the core of existing full-scope standards, but with valuation and custody folded into the new ALTS handbook.

Note that NAV is not AUM. This means the “illusion” of leverage gets stripped out of the tiers — a high-leverage fund and a low-leverage fund with the same NAV should bear similar regulatory costs. This is the biggest methodological change since AIFMD 2013.

02 | Why “relaxation”? The post-Brexit regulatory divergence

Set the new plan next to the EU’s latest AIFMD II, and the picture is stark.

The EU’s AIFMD II took effect in April 2026. Its direction is tightening: expanding supervision of mandates delegated to non-EU managers, requiring stricter liquidity management tools (LMTs), and requiring prime brokers to disclose material loss details to AIFMs. In effect, the EU is compressing the 2013 rules further toward “crisis defense.”

The UK’s new plan is the opposite. Latham & Watkins’ legal analysis directly used the phrase “material divergence.” Concrete examples:

· Leverage calculation — the EU requires AIFMs to report under both the “gross method” and the “commitment method”; the UK changes this to “you pick the appropriate method”;

· Custodian duties — the EU is strengthening custody duties; the UK moves 15 custodian requirements from legislation into the FCA handbook, easing the burden on custodians;

· Reporting frequency — the EU keeps the detailed Annex IV reporting; the UK replaces it with the new FRAME framework, targeting a 75% burden reduction;

· Remuneration code — the EU keeps mandatory deferral for “material risk takers”; the UK’s CP26/27 shifts to a “principles-based” approach, removing mandatory deferral and mandatory committees.

Put the two lines together: one is moving toward “post-crisis defense,” the other toward “growth orientation.” Once the UK’s new plan takes effect in 2028, European HFs wanting to enter the UK market or buy products from UK fund managers will face two sets of rules simultaneously.

💡 Headhunter view: the regulatory divergence isn’t just the word “relief.” For HF teams split across London, Singapore and Tokyo, it means compliance architecture costs are being reshuffled. If a fund has 80% of its assets in the UK and 20% in the EU, it will likely re-evaluate where its “master fund” should be registered once the new rules take effect. From the PMs we’ve recently spoken to, three are already seriously considering a “master fund + UK side letter” structure.

03 | Who are the biggest winners? Asian HFs’ London offices

The most notable winners of this reform aren’t Citadel or Millennium — they’re the HFs with large London offices but compliance structures mainly based in Asia.

According to the FCA consultation paper, the UK’s alternative asset management market totals £2 trillion with overall AUM of £16 trillion — the world’s second largest. A sizable share comes from Asian HFs’ London arms — Man Group’s Man GLG (London/Hong Kong dual headquarters), the UK compliance handled by Point72’s Hong Kong office, and Brevan Howard’s capital movement between ADGM and London.

The new plan means three things for them:

First, medium AIFMs will proliferate. The old full-scope threshold was €500 million AUM; the new medium tier is £750m-£5bn NAV. Many HFs that were full-scope but actually have NAV of only £500m-£1bn will automatically fall into the medium tier — with compliance costs expected to be cut 30-50%.

Second, leverage rules are loosened. The FCA itself deleted the unified “gross method” and “commitment method” reporting requirements, replacing them with AIFM self-declaration. That means UK funds can redesign their leverage structures — good news for multi-strategy, relative value and global macro teams.

Third, small AIFM exemptions expand. Small funds with NAV <£750m are exempt from detailed valuation, an independent risk function and several other requirements. This is effectively a “birth certificate” for early-stage HFs and startup multi-strategy teams — where full-scope compliance once cost £2-5 million a year, it could now drop to £0.5-1 million.

04 | Connecting with the rest of the week: from regulation to blow-ups to mega fundraising

Put the UK AIFMD reform into the week’s global HF context, and the picture completes.

Start with the US: per Bloomberg’s August 5 report, Citadel’s flagship fund Wellington rose 5.9% in July — almost entirely from a single trade: on July 29, Citadel bought the public stock portfolio of Situational Awareness at about a 10% discount, beating competing bids from Jane Street and Millennium. That pushed Citadel’s YTD gains to 12%. Around the same time, Millennium was in talks to raise $20 billion, Paloma cut its PM team in half, AB Arya shut down, and Jain Global returned external capital to move it under Millennium’s internal management.

The logic linking these events: “capital and talent are concentrating in mega platforms.” The UK’s AIFMD reform is the regulatory mirror of that same logic — cutting regulatory costs for small HFs isn’t because regulators suddenly changed their minds, but because big platforms are the players that will actually survive the industry’s next decade.

FCA’s Director of Market Oversight, Simon Walls, used an unusual phrase in the announcement: “a smarter regulator, which is more efficient and effective.” Translated into plain language: we intend to let the strong get stronger, and the weak stop wasting our time.

05 | Three deadlines: must-read for practitioners

This reform isn’t “pie in the sky”; it has a clear timetable. Three dates practitioners need to remember:

· September 16 — consultation closes on the CP26/27 remuneration code reform, affecting deferred bonus mechanics at every UK HF;

· September 22 — consultation closes on the new FRAME reporting framework, directly relevant to all funds managing more than £500 million of NAV;

· October 14 — feedback closes on the CP26/28 main package plus the Treasury’s SI draft; this is the most critical date — any detail you want preserved in the rules gets raised here.

The FCA has made clear that rule-revocation-type changes may take effect before 2028. In other words, from H2 2027, UK HFs could be the first to enjoy the regulatory dividend. For practitioners weighing whether to move their fund entity to Luxembourg, Dublin or Singapore, this is a moment worth pausing to re-run the numbers.
