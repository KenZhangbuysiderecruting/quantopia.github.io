Yen Jumps 3% in Two Days as 77,000 Short Contracts Hang Over Hedge Funds: Who Runs First Before the 9/18 BOJ?

2026-09-07 · Ken Zhang / Gravitas Recruitment

One widely repeated line needs to be corrected: this yen surge was not a Bank of Japan buying spree.

The Two-Day Move, and Who Actually Did the Buying

On September 3 and 4, the yen rose nearly 3% against the dollar over two trading sessions — the largest two-day gain since August 2024. USD/JPY was dragged from above 160 down to around 156, a one-month high. The first market reaction was "the Ministry of Finance intervened again." But Bloomberg compared the BoJ's published current-account forecast against dealer estimates — the gap is too small to support a "large-scale buy" conclusion.

What actually lifted the yen was the stampede itself. Masayuki Nakajima, senior strategist at Mizuho in London, put it bluntly: "The main driver of this yen rally was hedge-fund accounts closing out their own yen shorts."

The Fuse: Ueda and Takata Go Hawkish

The fuse was BoJ Governor Kazuo Ueda's hawkish remarks on September 2, with board member Hajime Takata adding fuel. The interest-rate swap market immediately priced in a 25-basis-point hike at the September 18 policy meeting (lifting the policy rate from 1.0% to 1.25%) as "near-certain," and is betting on nearly three more hikes before July of next year — a clear acceleration versus the average two-per-year pace seen since 2024.

The carry traders who borrowed yen to buy U.S. Treasuries, Brazilian real, and Mexican peso got hit square in the face: as long as the yen does not strengthen, the interest-rate differential is free money; the moment it rises, whoever runs first tramples whoever runs second.

The CFTC Data Tells a "Run, Then Return" Story

CFTC positioning data tells a "ran, then came back" story.

  • June 30: Leveraged-fund yen net short of about 138,000 contracts — the most crowded level since 2007. USD/JPY briefly broke 162; the yen was at its weakest level unseen since 1986.
  • Late April to late May: Japan's Ministry of Finance had already spent about ¥11.73 trillion (roughly $72.7 billion) supporting the currency, and again in late July and early August ran a coordinated intervention with the United States.
  • Week of August 4: Net short shrank to about 63,600 contracts, halved within a few weeks.
  • Week of August 25: It climbed back to about 77,000 contracts.

That last line is key. Per JPMorgan's quoting of CFTC data, leveraged funds held a net short yen position of 77,042 contracts as of the week of August 25 — note that Bloomberg-quoted media cite a different reading of 81,619 contracts. The gap comes from different statistical coverage, but both point to the same fact: the crowded short has not truly cleared; some used the rebound to reload shorts.

The latest official print (CFTC release on September 4, for the week of September 1) shows the yen remains a net short of 92,227 contracts. The short book is still hanging high.

Who's Running, and Who's Still Holding

Who is running is already clear: hedge funds. Chicago Mercantile Exchange data shows that on September 4, the trading volume of yen call options expiring this month was more than 2.5 times that of puts — traders are scrambling to buy calls to hedge the shorts they have not yet closed. Sagar Sambrani, head of FX options at Nomura London, gave the verdict: "The easy carry-trade era is over." Japanese exporters are also seizing the moment to sell dollars for yen, stoking the rally.

Those who haven't run are partly betting that intervention only "buys time." After USD/JPY fell from above 160 to around 156 on the week, Société Générale warned: to truly break the carry trade, the BoJ needs to deliver a "clearly hawkish" dose, and that bar is high. Others may simply have not made it in time.

JPMorgan's Tally: $100B+ Still in the Open

JPMorgan ran the numbers: the notional exposure of the remaining yen short is still estimated at more than $100 billion. If USD/JPY breaks below 155, that could trigger a new wave of accelerated short-covering — short-trampling-short is never a slow affair.

Three Scenarios, Three Paths

Three scenarios, three paths:

Scenario 1: 25bp hike + hawkish guidance. Shorts clear faster, the yen tests 155 or lower. This is the longs' preferred script — and the carry traders' worst fear.

Scenario 2: Hike, but no clear guidance. Shorts retreat slowly, volatility extends into the October meeting, and the market keeps guessing.

Scenario 3: Hold. Swap markets currently price the hike at above 60%, but not 100%. If the BoJ really does nothing, the yen retreats, those holding the short pick up an extra life, and those who added shorts reload.

Nomura has an even more aggressive scenario: if the yen weakens again toward 160, back-to-back hikes in October and December are "not impossible."

Why This Matters Beyond FX

For macro and FX traders, September 18 is not the finish line — it is the starting gun. Once the rate path confirms acceleration, yen-related hedging costs, cross-border capital flows, and the funding books in emerging-market high-yielders all need to reprice. Such structural-shift windows are usually when macro and FX teams expand the most aggressively — and when FX factor models inside quant funds suffer the most: there is no "continuously hiking Japan" in the historical sample.

The yen question will be answered on September 18.
