CICC's $500 Million: Buying Chinese Quant, With One Extra Layer

2026-11-15 · Ken Zhang / Gravitas Recruitment

The CSI 500 is down 11% this year. Over the same stretch, the hedge fund portfolio CICC runs with its own capital returned 12.7% annualised, without a single losing year. Put those two numbers side by side and a cheerful conclusion writes itself. Hold on.

Bloomberg reported on 11 November that CICC aims to launch a $500 million fund of hedge funds as early as this month, focused on Greater China and Asia-Pacific equity strategies. It is run by the capital advisory solutions team inside the bank's equities division — set up in 2016 and focused on Chinese and global hedge fund manager research ever since.

The phrase that matters is "the first one open to outside investors". Everything before this was proprietary capital.

Where the 12.7% stops

Start with provenance: the number comes from marketing material shown to prospective investors. It describes allocations made with the team's own capital between July 2023 and August 2026 — 12.7% annualised, no down year in the period.

Three things it does not say: whether it was independently audited, whether it is net of all fees, and whether a new fund would replicate the historical allocations.

Proprietary money and client money are different instruments. With your own capital, a 20% drawdown brings no phone call and no redemption risk; you can wait. Client capital arrives with lock-ups, redemption terms and a quarterly call. The same methodology tends to behave differently on the two.

This is not nitpicking. The entire product is the claim "we have been screening managers for a decade" — so the quality of that record is the product.

Four routes into Chinese quant, and this is the fourth

Route one is a direct mandate. Earlier this year, Meridian & Saturn Capital won a $1 billion China equity mandate from a Middle Eastern sovereign wealth fund, run market-neutral.

Route two is building your own book. Qube Research & Technologies expanded its China long-only strategy more than tenfold in the year to January.

Route three is standing up a local research team and growing capability slowly. The slowest, and the most expensive.

CICC is taking route four: don't pick the managers yourself — buy a portfolio that a local team picks for you. One extra layer.

What that layer actually adds is worth pulling apart.

It is not buying returns, it is buying diligence

500 funds screened a year, 10% assessed in depth — call it fifty — and fifteen to twenty held. Read the numbers backwards: nine managers in ten never enter a foreign allocator's field of view, and nine in ten of those never reach the diligence stage.

For an allocator without a local research team, that layer saves work. It does not have to judge how much excess return is left in Chinese quant, or work out the capacity limits of index-enhanced, market-neutral and CTA strategies separately.

For a mid-sized Chinese manager, the same layer is a ceiling. Whether you sit inside those fifty determines whether you appear in a foreign investor's memo at all — and that rarely turns on returns. It turns on three harder things: how complete your operational due diligence file is, how your performance is disclosed, and whether you can explain your strategy in English.

One detail worth noticing: roughly half the money goes to "fundamental equity and quant". That split is the attitude in numbers. Foreign capital does not disbelieve Chinese quant; it does not entirely believe it either. Half the book is reserved for people who can explain a company.

The fee layering is what this structure costs

A fund of funds carries an extra fee layer by construction. IOSCO, assessing this product type, names manager due diligence, portfolio concentration and multiple fee layers as the three core concerns. CICC's management fee, performance fee, minimum commitment and legal domicile have not been disclosed.

On the other side, excess returns in Chinese quant are compressing. As of 6 November, of the 314 quantitative long-only funds launched in 2026 with disclosed data, 234 were underwater since inception — 74.52% — with an average return of −8.11%.

Squeeze both ends and the question becomes concrete: what actually reaches the LP, net. That is not CICC's problem alone. Every China-focused fund of funds has to answer it.

There is also a timing coincidence. The fund may launch this month, with the CSI 500 down 11% year to date and MSCI Asia-Pacific up 11%. Raising against the index can mean you see something others don't — or simply that fundraising takes this long. A fund of funds runs eighteen months from first LP meeting to closing as a matter of course.

What a vehicle like this hires

The team structure differs from a hedge fund's. It does not manufacture alpha; it manufactures judgement.

Investment due diligence: able to read where a quant product's return comes from, ask whether the excess is factor-driven or execution-driven, and work through a several-dozen-item operational DDQ. These people are scarce in China and mostly come from securities-house FOF desks, bank wealth subsidiaries or offshore funds of funds.

Portfolio management: allocate and rebalance across fifteen to twenty underlying funds — running a portfolio of managers. The sensitivity that matters here is to correlation, capacity and redemption liquidity, more than to return.

Investor relations: the target LPs are endowments and family offices in Hong Kong, Europe, the US and the Middle East. Bilingual is the floor; you also need to handle Middle East time zones and European compliance questionnaires.

All three share one trait: none requires you to make money better than the next person. All require you to judge who is making money better. In China's CV pool, that is far rarer than a factor engineer.

If your firm were to serve foreign LPs, how many people could independently complete an English operational due diligence questionnaire? The number is the datapoint worth reporting.
