Cooper Investors Cut Seven Funds to One: An Australian Veteran's Pullback

2026-09-30 · Ken Zhang / Gravitas Recruitment

On Monday night, Melbourne-based Cooper Investors made the sharpest decision in its 25-year history: it is closing six of its seven funds and keeping only the Brunswick Fund. Two portfolio managers, Justin O'Brien and Stuart McLachlan, are leaving, along with part of the headcount.

Why would a stockpicker built on "long-termism" pull back this hard after a quarter of a century?

The numbers first. Founded in 2001, Cooper runs global equities, employs more than 25 investment professionals, is 100% employee-owned, and counts Australian pension funds, superannuation schemes, state government agencies and school endowments among its clients — the most stability-sensitive money there is.

Its real problem was never size. It was return. Cooper Investors' Global Equities Fund (unhedged) returned 6.6% in the first quarter of FY2026, but the full financial year came in at -6.4%. More telling: about six percentage points of that -6.4% were a headwind from a rising Australian dollar — in local currency terms the fund was close to flat.

In other words, a fund that did not perform badly was pushed into cutting — by FX, and by the broader environment for active management.

That is what makes this worth a quant professional's attention. Australian active equity funds have had a hard few years: fees compressed by index funds, investors voting with their feet toward passive, and less room every year for local stockpickers. Peter Cooper's choice looks less like a performance accident and more like an old firm narrowing its front and concentrating resources on its strongest strategy.

The founder deserves a paragraph of his own. Peter Cooper is a fixture of Australian value investing, a long-time proponent of a culture in which "humility and excellence" coexist, and a founding supporter of the Hearts & Minds investment platform. He is not a manager who went viral on a single strategy; he is a veteran who ground it out over 25 years of discipline. When a founder cuts six of his own products, that is itself a signal — he no longer believes in the "spread wide across many products" logic.

Closing six to keep one looks like contraction; the core of it is concentration. Brunswick is the flagship of flagships, and pushing all research firepower back into that single fund is a classic pullback of the fist rather than a retreat from the fight.

From a recruiter's seat there is a more practical implication: with two PMs leaving, Australia's active equity lane has just put two experienced portfolio managers into the mobility pool. When a 25-year-old shop has to cut product lines to protect itself, the industry signal is clear — demand for active management talent is shifting from "people who can launch new funds" to "people who can take one core fund deep". The hiring brief has changed.

For Quantopia readers, what is worth keeping is not the fate of one Australian fund but the global pattern it exposes: active management is going through a capacity reduction. Australia is just the part above the waterline. China's private fund industry has spent 2026 with scale and excess returns pulling in opposite directions — the 10bn-RMB quant cohort has grown to 61 firms, while excess return on index-enhanced products has been broadly compressed. Australian stockpickers are cutting product lines; Chinese quant firms are rebuilding research. Behind both is the same thing: money is getting smarter, and firms can only concentrate firepower where real alpha lives.

A veteran who has run money for 25 years and still cuts six of his own funds deserves more serious attention than a manager who is always launching something new.
