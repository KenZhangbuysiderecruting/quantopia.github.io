Elliott vs. Deutsche Telekom: The $300B Activist Strike That Paused Europe's Biggest Merger

2026-09-06 · Ken Zhang / Gravitas Recruitment

A $300 billion merger has been put on pause by an activist hedge fund whose position size has not been disclosed.

Elliott Investment Management took a stake in Deutsche Telekom on September 2, holding no more than 3% — precisely under the mandatory disclosure threshold set by Germany's securities law, WpHG §21. Elliott's message was one line: drop the merger, buy back more stock.

The largest public-market M&A deal in history has been held at the door by a position nobody can size. The merger pushed by Deutsche Telekom CEO Tim Höttges since April has seen the shares fall 9% over the past twelve months, with a market cap of roughly €138 billion. "German shareholders subsidizing American shareholders" is the core of the dispute.

  • Potential deal size: $300B — the largest merger in public-market history
  • DT's current stake in T-Mobile US: ~54%
  • DT share price, trailing 12 months: -9%
  • DT market cap: ~€138B
  • August buyback expanded to: €5B (~4% of shares outstanding)
  • Government holdings: federal government 14.1% + KfW 14.2% = 28%

Elliott's exact position size has not been publicly disclosed. Under WpHG §21, holdings below 3% require no filing.

Before Elliott even built the position, T-Mobile US executives had already told Deutsche Telekom they no longer supported the merger.

  • Semafor reported in July: T-Mobile executives view the merger as dilutive to shareholder returns and a trigger for CFIUS review
  • CFIUS condition: T-Mobile's US revenue must be reinvested in the United States — which would cut the multi-billion-dollar annual dividend Deutsche Telekom receives from T-Mobile
  • Some non-controlling shareholders in T-Mobile US, mostly large institutions, have already come out against the deal

In other words, Elliott is not fighting alone. T-Mobile's own executives and shareholders had already turned.

Before Elliott showed up, DT had already tried to rescue itself.

  • August 2026: DT expanded its 2026 buyback from €3B to €5B (roughly 4% of shares outstanding)
  • August 27: CEO Tim Höttges was still publicly pushing the merger
  • September 3, after Bloomberg broke the Elliott stake: DT shares rose 1.5-2.3%, T-Mobile US 2.82%

DT's play was "appease shareholders with buybacks." Elliott's play is "force buybacks with the merger threat." The two are on opposite sides of the same agenda item.

1. CFIUS (US): T-Mobile's US revenue must be invested domestically

2. EU foreign investment screening (FDI Regulation): the merger needs EU clearance

3. DOJ antitrust plus separate FCC approval

Layer on German domestic politics — unions, Berlin's political sign-off, and a federal government plus KfW stake of 28% that would be diluted to roughly 13% after the merger.

JPMorgan analyst Akhil Dattani wrote: "Elliott's entry will force DT to clarify the merger logic or formally abandon it."

Union Investment fund manager Matthias Lützen told Reuters:

"Elliott's entry and the recently expanded buyback make the merger less likely. Over the long term, Deutsche Telekom should continue focusing on buybacks. At the current valuation, an expanded buyback is also reasonable."

That view is not isolated — German domestic institutional shareholders have collectively lined up behind Elliott.

Deutsche Telekom is not Elliott's only target. This week the FT reported that Elliott has simultaneously built a position in France's Air Liquide.

Europe is Elliott's new front. The firm's activist book is expanding across the US, Europe and Asia-Pacific, driven by a dual window: European asset valuations at a discount since 2025, and a governance-reform cycle.

Compare that with Chinese outbound M&A. Between 2024 and 2026, state-linked buyers such as CIC and the Silk Road Fund have faced the same shareholder-return pressure.

  • State capital and private or foreign capital are measured against different return benchmarks
  • Cross-border deals increasingly require a rebuilt shareholder-return framework
  • Many failed Chinese overseas acquisitions (Chinalco, CREC, Maxam and others) were at bottom a clash between state-capital logic and commercial-capital logic

The mirror Elliott holds up is this: any cross-border merger will be re-examined through a shareholder-return lens — whether the shareholder is the German government, KfW, or a private fund.

A $300 billion merger has been paused by a position nobody can see.

This is not the ending. It is the beginning. Over the next three to six months, Deutsche Telekom has to respond formally: either abandon the merger and expand buybacks, or produce a version of the deal that can convince every stakeholder — the German government, KfW, T-Mobile US and institutional shareholders.

For a headhunter, the dispute sends two signals: ① cross-border M&A, legal and government-relations talent will keep commanding a premium; ② public-market activist styles are expanding in Europe, and hiring for those teams will follow.
