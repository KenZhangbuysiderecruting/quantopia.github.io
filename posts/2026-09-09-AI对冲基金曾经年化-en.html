AI Hedge Funds Once Earned an Extra 5% a Year — Then the Edge Vanished After 2017

2026-09-09 · Ken Zhang / Gravitas Recruitment

Can AI manage money? After a decade of argument, an NBER working paper has given the most evidence-based answer yet: it could — but only until 2017. The paper, "The Growth and Performance of Artificial Intelligence in Asset Management" (NBER Working Paper 35273, by researchers from the University of Melbourne, UT Austin and Southern Methodist University), does something methodical rather than flashy: it screens 116,000 SEC investment-adviser filings and the strategy descriptions of 7,896 US hedge funds from 2005 to 2024, using a large language model to separate funds where AI genuinely drives decisions from those that merely mention AI. The bar is deliberately high. A fund only counts as AI-driven if its strategy description explicitly references AI used for predictive modelling and trading-signal generation; generic words like "quantitative," "systematic" or "algorithmic" do not qualify. Under that strict test, only about 1.1% of the funds in the sample were ever classified as AI-driven. The results come in three parts. Before 2017, AI-driven funds genuinely outperformed. Risk-adjusted, they beat their non-AI peers by roughly 50 basis points a month — about 5% a year. That does not sound like much, but in the hedge fund world it is real alpha. After 2017, the edge disappeared — statistically indistinguishable from zero — and has kept decaying by roughly 6 basis points a year since. The most interesting part is not that the edge decayed, but how. Your first instinct is that the early cohort was genuinely good and a flood of less capable imitators dragged the average down. The data does not support that story — the earliest adopters lost the edge too. One more detail is worth noting. Within the same adviser, AI funds still outperform their non-AI sibling funds by about 35 basis points a month. The authors read this cautiously: either the technique does something, or it is quietly consuming the adviser's other funds. The most sobering finding sits at the money end. Investors never paid for the "AI label" — flows followed realised performance, not strategy documents that mention machine learning. Put plainly: the AI story in asset management is an "early-adopter dividend," not a "permanent edge." The years it worked may simply have been the years few people were using it; once everyone piles in with compute and models, the excess is competed away. It is the same ending as every crowded strategy — AI just made the process faster. The retail mirror is even more direct. AIEQ, an AI-run stock-picking ETF with a ten-year track record, returned 4.5% annualised over the five years to June 2026, versus 13.4% for the S&P 500 total return index. Another NBER paper goes further: when ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini were asked to build portfolios themselves, they loaded up on semiconductors (41% of holdings on average, versus 21% in the S&P 500) with an average beta near 1.6 — beating the S&P only by betting on big tech, with no edge left after trading costs. Their stock picks were driven, to a striking degree, by who made the most headlines. Put the two papers together and they converge on a single sentence: AI is an amplifier, not a money printer. It amplifies your research speed, your discipline, your understanding of markets. It cannot amplify what you do not have. For anyone evaluating a fund or a person, that is also a reminder: do not judge by whether "AI" appears on the slides. Ask how the performance was produced — and whether it can last.
