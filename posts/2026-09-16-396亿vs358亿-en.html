$39.6B vs $35.8B: A Market Maker Out-Earned JPMorgan, So the Bank Hired a Professor

2026-09-16 · Ken Zhang / Gravitas Recruitment

Last month, JPMorgan handed a brand-new high-frequency trading lab to someone who has never held a full-time trading seat — and gave him a Managing Director title.

His name is Xi Chen. For the past decade and a half, his home turf was a lecture hall at NYU Stern and a lab at Amazon. Not a trading floor.

The people he now has to compete with are locked in at roughly $2M a year per head by firms like Jane Street and Citadel Securities.

Start with the person.

Xi Chen holds a PhD from Carnegie Mellon, is a professor at NYU Stern and an Andre Meyer Faculty Fellow, and made Forbes 30 Under 30 in 2017. His fields: machine learning, high-dimensional statistics and operations research. He has published more than 120 papers and three books, and spent two years as a principal scientist at Amazon working on forecasting, pricing and recommendation systems.

In August, an internal JPMorgan memo confirmed his appointment as a Managing Director in the Quantitative Trading and Research (QTR) group, reporting to Chi Nzelu.

On September 12, he posted on LinkedIn with unusual specificity about who he wants: people with full-time HFT experience, or people with deep AI pre-training experience. And one line worth noting — for standout senior candidates, an Associate-level posting can become VP or Director.

A bank is now using a professor's ruler to redefine what "trading talent" means.

The team is called AI Markets Lab. It sits inside QTR, with 12 openings across New York, London and Singapore covering signal research, pricing, market making, execution, portfolio construction, risk management and production systems — a complete systematic trading pipeline.

The keyword is not AI. It is market making. The job description is blunt: researchers are expected to "design, backtest, and implement market-making and risk-taking strategies." Engineering roles require low-latency C++.

In plain terms: JPMorgan is going to trade high- and mid-frequency itself.

Why now?

Jane Street moved into US Treasury market making. Citadel Securities moved into equity block trading. Per Reuters, Jane Street's 2025 net trading revenue reached roughly $39.6B, against about $35.8B for JPMorgan's trading division — the market maker had overtaken the bank.

In April, Jamie Dimon named Citadel Securities a "new competitor" in his annual shareholder letter. Earlier, JPMorgan had trimmed part of its fixed-income financing to Jane Street.

When your client starts doing your business, you can no longer treat it only as a client.

One number deserves its own line. Per eFinancialCareers, average per-head pay at Jane Street and Citadel Securities exceeded $2M last year — a figure that already includes graduates and back-office staff. It is higher than the total package of most bank MDs. Those are the people JPMorgan is trying to hire.

As a quant headhunter, I have always been skeptical about whether a bank can do high-frequency trading well. It is not a technology problem. It is an organizational one.

A bank's hand is resources: global clients, institutional order flow, balance sheet, cross-asset products. HFT is a different game — infrastructure, latency, and people who are used to a pure trading environment and do not want to be constrained by bank process.

Can a professor who spent more than a decade in academia build, inside a bank's structure, a team that genuinely competes with Jane Street and Citadel Securities? That is the real open question here.

For candidates, the signal is clear: HFT, market making, low-latency C++ and machine learning just gained another buyer. A bank re-entering the space means one more party willing to pay up for those skills.

And this fight is not only in New York. Per secondary media reports, JPMorgan relocated more than 30 China-based quant staff from Beijing and Shanghai to Singapore and Hong Kong in July. The talent war is spreading across Asia.

One question for the quants reading this: if a bank and a market maker both hand you an offer, and the market maker pays 30% more in total comp — who do you choose?
