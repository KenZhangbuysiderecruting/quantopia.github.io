18 Months, 0.2 Correlation: Does the AI-Dug Factor Hold Up?

2026-11-25 · Ken Zhang / Gravitas Recruitment

One institution says 18 months. The industry's rule of thumb says six.

What circulated last week was this: at an internal meeting, a top Chinese quant told the room it had a factor found end-to-end by a large language model, with no human researcher in the loop, that had beaten its benchmark in live trading for more than 18 months while correlating below 0.2 with conventionally built factors. If true, it overturns two things at once — the industry's experience that factors decay in about six months, and the comfortable view that AI is merely a productivity tool.

The account reached National Business Daily through Dou Changmin, general manager of Beijing Quantitative Investment Management. He called the live record highly credible and said the point was that the factor escaped the logical frame a human researcher would have imposed. In the same article, two other institutions were considerably more reserved.

Eighteen months asks "how varied", not "how long"

Those 18 months run roughly from the second half of 2025 through 2026. And the second half of this year brought a style reversal in A-shares: growth sold off hard, the market stayed oversold for a long stretch, and the short-term reversal did not deliver a meaningful recovery. A top Shanghai quant's reading is that the drawdown belongs to that market environment and has no direct link to whether AI was used in research.

On the surface that defends AI. It actually points somewhere harder: a factor beating its benchmark for 18 months may simply have grown into the shape of that particular tape. Tiansuan Quant put it more directly — if a model quietly absorbs the market style that was winning during training, nobody notices until the style turns, and then the drawdown gets amplified.

So the question about 18 months is not whether it is long enough. It is whether it is varied enough.

"No human" — which part, exactly

Between an idea and a live factor there are at least four points where a person leaves a mark: which data to use, what objective to set, where to put the risk thresholds, and when to declare the factor dead. The Shanghai firm's point is that AI can complete an assigned mining task, but talking about task completion while setting aside output quality and reliability is of limited use.

If "no human in the loop" means the whole pipeline, it describes an organisation that does not need researchers. If it means only the generation step, it describes a tool. Those are different orders of magnitude, and the report does not say which one is meant.

Same for the 0.2. Measured against the firm's own legacy factor library, 0.2 means the factor diversifies its existing book. Measured against a public industry-wide factor library, 0.2 is the story. The first is internal optimisation; only the second breaks the rule of thumb. No comparison basis was given — and that gap matters more than the 18 months, because "low correlation" is the most reused phrase in this kind of narrative and almost never arrives with a definition attached.

Is AI responsible for the drawdown

On one side, a factor with no human involvement beating its benchmark for 18 months. On the other, products down to RMB 0.6 NAV inside six months. These are not contradictory; they are two different samples — one strategy's self-report, and an industry distribution in which other firms were making money at the same time.

All three firms converge on one point: AI is a research tool, and tool adoption does not mean converging results. Tiansuan argues that signals moving from scarce to ubiquitous is the likely path, and that factors which are easy to copy get compressed by competition until the excess return thins. The Shanghai firm expects AI to push information into prices faster while intensifying competition in research.

Put plainly: AI dilutes the signal, not the gap between firms.

What it means if you work in this seat

The point of this story for practitioners is not whether it is true. It is how it changes hiring. If model-driven factor discovery becomes accepted practice, researcher value moves to two ends: problem definition and data definitions on one side — deciding what the model should look for and why its findings should be believed — and risk exposure plus crowding assessment on the other, judging when a set of signals fails together. The middle layer, iterating factors inside a given framework, compresses fastest.

When the three firms explained the drawdown, they landed almost identically on risk exposure, crowding, turnover, leverage and execution — not on whether AI was involved. That landing point is what these seats are now measured on.

Boundaries

All three figures come from an account of an internal meeting, relayed by a third party, with no product monthly report, no attribution report and no comparison basis. Dou considers it credible; the other two firms doubt the premise. Both attitudes are reasonable, because the verifiable part is genuinely thin.

The RMB 0.6 and 0.2 NAV figures are a Wind industry distribution and cannot be mapped back to any specific firm.

To test the factor, three questions are enough: where the worst drawdown fell within those 18 months; which factor library the 0.2 was measured against; and what the turnover and position concentration were. Answer all three and it is a method. Fail, and it is still a narrative.
