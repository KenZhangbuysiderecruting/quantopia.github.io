The Biggest Door Into Quant Isn't Research: 747 JDs Show 4x More Engineering Roles

2026-10-11 · Ken Zhang / Gravitas Recruitment

Every autumn recruiting season, the question most people ask is: how do I get into quant research? But put 747 real job postings from 21 top firms over the past quarter into one table, and a counterintuitive result appears — the role the quant industry is hiring for at scale is not researcher at all.

First, the structure. Of 747 postings, software engineering accounts for 311 (42%), quantitative research 78 (10%), quantitative trading 69 (9%), and machine learning/AI 44 (6%).

Engineering roles outnumber research roles four to one.

What this means for job seekers comes down to four mismatches.

Mismatch 1: everyone crowds into research; the industry is hiring engineering.

That research seats are competitive is an open secret. But the ratio — 78 research seats to 311 engineering seats — is something most people don't know. The reason isn't hard to guess: research sits closer to "managing money" and reads like the top of the pyramid. What the hiring side actually needs is people who can build microsecond-level systems and keep data pipelines stable.

The screening rulers are entirely different too. Among the 78 research postings, Python appears in 87%, statistics and probability 62%, C++ 54%, machine learning 53%, and 46% explicitly require a PhD — nearly half. Engineering roles weight systems capability: Linux/Unix 41%, distributed systems and concurrency 31%, networking and operating systems 31%. Research screens for "degree + math + modeling." Engineering screens for "systems + performance + engineering habits."

Mismatch 2: the volume is in experienced hiring, not campus recruiting.

Of the 747 postings, internships are 7%, entry-level 3%, and senior/management 18%. Among the 325 JDs that mention years of experience, the median minimum is 5 years. Campus recruiting gets the loudest coverage, but in stock terms, the industry is hiring people who can contribute immediately. This explains the common complaint of "sent hundreds of applications, heard nothing back" — those roles were never designed for new graduates.

Mismatch 3: the most valuable skills appear least often in JDs.

The report keeps a "rare but valuable" list: KDB+/q appeared 3 times all quarter, DPDK (kernel bypass) 3 times, FIX protocol 5 times, Scala 3 times, reinforcement learning 4 times, JAX 4 times. Very few people know them; the people who need them are in a hurry — the core skills of market making and low-latency systems hide in those few lines. Conversely, Python at 53% is the number-one hard skill, but it is an entry ticket, not a moat.

One more signal in passing: LLMs have entered quant JDs at 6% occurrence, concentrated in ML/AI roles (about half of that category); traditional research roles haven't adopted them widely yet. For anyone trying to position on AI skills, the window is still open.

Mismatch 4: geographically, APAC is not the main battleground.

By city, posting counts run: London 136, New York 103, Chicago 60, Hong Kong 30, Singapore 26. London and New York together approach half the total; Hong Kong and Singapore combined don't add up to London alone. This doesn't mean APAC doesn't matter — it means if you only apply to Hong Kong and Singapore, your pool is narrower than you think. London's density as "the European quant hub" is routinely underestimated by Asian job seekers.

Back to the opening question: how do you get into quant? This data's answer is not "go grind for a research seat." It is: first see clearly what the industry is actually hiring for and which ruler it screens with. Research wants a PhD and mathematical depth; engineering wants systems and performance instincts. Experienced hiring dwarfs campus recruiting. And the most valuable skills are the niche trading-system ones.

Knowing what cards you hold matters more than knowing what the top of the pyramid looks like.
