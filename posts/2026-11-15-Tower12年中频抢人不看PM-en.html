Tower's Twelve-Year Hire: Recruiting Has Stopped Looking at the PM

2026-11-15 · Ken Zhang / Gravitas Recruitment

On 3 November, Tower Research created a new seat: head of mid-frequency trading strategies. The hire had spent twelve years at Millennium, mostly running algorithmic execution technology, with a final year in a liquidity provision technology role. Before that, seven years building algorithmic systems at Bank of America.

eFinancialCareers reported it first, Hedgeweek followed. The appointment itself is not complicated. What is complicated is the timing, and its relationship to a reverse hire six months earlier.

Not a poaching war — two species swapping organs

Three moves: two toward Millennium, one toward Tower. It reads like a tug of war. What is actually moving is different.

Millennium took the person who ran low-latency infrastructure and a co-head of credit trading. What a platform multistrategy lacks is infrastructure that can carry dozens of pods, and people who can stand up a credit pod independently.

Tower took an algorithmic execution technology head to run mid-frequency strategy. In proprietary trading firms, leaders in that direction usually grow from inside — off the execution desk, out of an engineering group, up one strategy line. This time it came from outside.

What mid-frequency is, and why the seat is empty

Mid-frequency holds positions for minutes to days. High frequency is microseconds to milliseconds.

The two horizons demand entirely different things. High frequency competes on latency, matching engines, distance to the exchange, FPGAs. Mid-frequency competes on machine learning, data quality, compute density. The first is an engineering problem. The second is a research problem plus an engineering problem.

Iain Dunning, head of AI at Hudson River Trading, said last year — and the line gets quoted constantly — that the latency has largely been arbitraged away. That explains why Tower, Jane Street and HRT have all pushed into mid-frequency in recent years. Not ambition: diminishing returns in the microsecond band.

Mid-frequency happens to need exactly what the industry has spent three years building: machine learning teams, data platforms, GPU clusters. Whoever built that infrastructure has earned the right to go mid-frequency. Tower has been building for years. Now it needs someone to translate that into strategy.

Technologist to strategy head is a rare path

Read the CV and the nineteen years sit on a single line: how to get an order out, faster, and less visibly. Seven years of algorithmic development at Bank of America, twelve years of algorithmic execution technology at Millennium, a final year in liquidity provision technology.

In most firms, execution technology and strategy research are two separate groups. Mid-frequency stitches them together. The return on a mid-frequency strategy may come from a better execution model or from a better signal, and often you cannot tell which did the work.

So the seat wants someone who reads model evaluation and matching latency with equal fluency. Very few people match, because the best in each skill usually do not sit in the same group.

Asia shows this more starkly. At most proprietary firms with an Asian presence, mid-frequency is still at the "move the strategy over from head office" stage — the local hires implement, they do not define. The difference is practical: wherever the defining happens, that is where the strategy iterates. A city that only takes implementation will still be taking implementation three years from now.

New York got a defining seat. Whether this replicates to Hong Kong or Singapore in the next six months depends on whether anyone locally can speak both model and production. In Asia-Pacific, that population is counted in single digits.

What it means on each side of the table

If you work in execution technology or low-latency engineering: mid-frequency is a real lateral route. Your skills are not support there; they are part of where the return comes from. Three years ago that route was invisible. Now the seats have names.

If you do machine learning research: what mid-frequency wants is not publication record but the ability to land a model in production and align it with execution. Both requirements tend to appear in the same job description, and very few candidates have genuinely done both.

If you are hiring: do not scope the search to the same function at peer firms. Tower's hire came out of the technology side of a multistrategy hedge fund, not from another proprietary shop. Scope by capability, not by industry — otherwise you search the market and miss the person.

How large is your firm's mid-frequency team, and how many of them can do both model evaluation and production delivery — matching, latency, the lot? That split is the number worth reporting.
