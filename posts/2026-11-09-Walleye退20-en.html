Walleye Hands Back $2 Billion as 108 Pods Close

November 9, 2026 · Ken Zhang / Gravitas Recruitment

On November 5, Walleye Capital wrote to clients: about $2 billion is coming back. The hedge fund shrinks to roughly $10 billion — essentially where it started the year. Another $5 billion stays in Dockside, its separately managed accounts business. The same day, Bloomberg reported that Anil Gondi, head of equities, had left along with a substantial portion of the equities teams. In October the fund lost 5.9%, its worst month since March 2020, taking the year-to-date decline to 6.8%.

The four numbers form a chain. The first two explain why Walleye is returning money. The last two explain why, right now, the hiring news matters more than the market news.

Where the growth stopped

Walleye managed about $4bn at the end of 2023 and grew to roughly $17bn including Dockside — the archetypal challenger. The ten large multi-strategy firms tracked by PivotalPath held $2.2 trillion including borrowed money at the end of 2025, more than triple the 2018 figure. Capital is concentrating in the giants led by Millennium and Citadel; second-tier firms trade growth for a place at the table.

CEO and CIO Will England was direct in the letter. The internal equity long-short business had been the firm's weakest strategy since 2023 and had substantially underperformed the external long-short book. New leadership had been brought in; it did not deliver. Meanwhile the volatility business had its strongest month on record in October and was on track for its best year in dollar terms.

So the $2bn going back maps to the weak unit, and the resources go to the strong ones: volatility, quant and external allocations. England called it a proactive decision. Walleye is not exiting equity long-short — it keeps exposure through external managers and a handful of highest-conviction internal stock pickers. His words: the firm's competitive advantage does not lie in replicating the traditional multi-manager equities model.

From hire to exit in under eighteen months

Gondi joined Walleye in 2025 after a year of gardening leave following his departure from a large multi-strategy platform. From the appointment to the dismantling of the unit: under eighteen months. The same batch of reporting carries two more short-cycle cases. David Heinau joined Brevan Howard in December 2025 and left in this round, after eight years running proprietary trading at TD Securities. The shorter the tenure, the less history a firm feels obliged to carry when it resets a business line.

108 pods and the 8% line

eFinancialCareers reported on November 6, citing Bloomberg data: on performance through October, many funds were already below Ken Griffin's viability line of the risk-free rate plus 4%. With three-month Treasury yields where they are, that line sits above 8%. Mike Alfaro, CIO of Gallo Partners, put a number on the damage: 108 pods have closed.

Alfaro is part of the same chain. Recruiter calls keep coming, all asking the same question — are you still hiring. The resumes, he says, are plentiful. He does not expect a V-shaped recovery, and he thinks the industry will reprice what portfolio management means: managers who sold themselves as market neutral while trading market direction are being marked down.

Three quarters, from a $15m sign-on to unemployed

The sharpest single case: a senior PM who ran a supposedly fully hedged market-neutral book, up 14% early in the year, fielding offers from major multi-strategy firms at $15m sign-on bonuses while holding out for a $6m year-end bonus. By the end of October the book was down 9% and he was out. From holding out for a better offer to unemployed in under three quarters.

The banks are the counter-data point. Per Tricumen, revenue per head on equities desks is up more than 30%, and big bonuses are expected. But the pool of displaced PMs is growing, and Alfaro's read is that an oversupply of talent pushes both pay and hiring the wrong way.

The same word on both sides of the Pacific

Bloomberg reported on November 6, citing data compiled by China Merchants Futures: domestic long-only quant strategies lost more than 10% in October, turning the year to date into a loss of at least 6%. Market-neutral products, normally hedged with shorts, lost 3% to 5% for the month. The CSI 300 fell 8.5% in October, its worst month in more than a decade.

Pick the business line first, then the firm

Walleye's letter does not read like a retreat. It reads like a rotation — out of the crowded strategy, into the ones where the firm claims a differentiated right to win. For anyone weighing an offer, the ordering it implies is plain: choose the business line before the brand. A firm's name cannot carry a unit that gets reset wholesale; a business line with a real edge finds an exit wherever it sits.

If you follow this market, the useful datapoint is mobility, not the headline loss. How long displaced PMs stay out is the cleanest read on how tight the top of the talent market still is.
